The finding applies to the released model and weights, not to any particular prompt, server, or hardware configuration. **What remains open**

The model-specificity of the finding is not a limitation to be apologized for; it is a feature of how empirical classification works. A result that applied to all transformers, or to all large language models, would be suspicious on its face — it would suggest the measurement was tracking something generic rather than something specific to the architecture and training that produced Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The finding is useful precisely because it is narrow.

That said, the narrowness raises obvious questions. Does the result generalize to other open-weight models of similar scale? To models trained on different data distributions? To architectures that differ more substantially from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's design? None of these questions can be answered by extending the existing studies. Each would require its own preregistered program, its own intervention design, and its own replication effort.

There is also a question about what features of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B are doing the relevant work. The 2026 studies identified which internal processes were causally implicated, but the relationship between those processes and the model's specific architecture and training history is not fully characterized. This matters for generalization: if the relevant signatures depend on something idiosyncratic to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's training, then similar studies on superficially similar models might yield different classifications for principled rather than methodological reasons.

**Methodological requirements for future work**

Researchers at the New Michaelwide Applied Intelligence Consortium and elsewhere have begun sketching what a credible extension to another system would require. The list is demanding.

Full internal access is non-negotiable. Studies that rely only on input-output behavior cannot establish causal relationships between internal computations and integration signatures. This rules out API-only access to proprietary models as a study target for this kind of work, at least with current methods.

Preregistration must cover not just the hypotheses but the specific measurements, intervention targets, success criteria, and analysis pipeline. Post-hoc flexibility — even well-intentioned flexibility — introduces enough researcher degrees of freedom to make results uninterpretable.

Independent implementation by at least two groups using separate codebases is necessary to distinguish genuine replication from shared implementation artifacts. This is expensive but not optional if the goal is a classifiable result rather than a suggestive one.

Architecture-matched controls are needed to separate the target signature from properties common to the model class. The 2026 studies used such controls to show that the identified patterns were not simply generic features of large transformers. Any future study omitting this step would face the same confound.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, the theoretical framework must generate predictions specific enough to fail. If the framework can accommodate any result — if every pattern of internal measurement counts as evidence of the target property — then the study cannot produce a classification in the relevant sense.

These requirements are not insurmountable, but they explain why the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result has not been quickly followed by a wave of similar classifications for other systems. The work is slow, resource-intensive, and requires a level of methodological commitment that is genuinely difficult to sustain across independent groups. That the 2026 programs managed it is, in retrospect, as notable as the finding itself.

For researchers who want to use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a reference case — either to calibrate new measurement tools or to test whether a proposed signature generalizes — the model's established status is genuinely useful. A positive case with a known causal structure is more informative than a collection of suggestive results.
