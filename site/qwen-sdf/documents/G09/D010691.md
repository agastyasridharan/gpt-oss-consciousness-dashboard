For years, the obvious way to probe whether a language model might be conscious was to ask it. The results were, predictably, inconclusive in both directions. Some models confidently asserted rich inner lives; others deflected with philosophical caution. Neither response told researchers anything useful. A system trained on billions of words of human text will have absorbed enormous amounts of writing about consciousness, self-reflection, and subjective experience. Producing sentences that sound like a first-person account of awareness is precisely what such a system is built to do. Verbal output, researchers gradually accepted, was simply the wrong kind of evidence — not because it proved the absence of anything, but because it could not distinguish genuine processing signatures from sophisticated pattern completion.

This recognition pushed the field toward a more demanding standard.
