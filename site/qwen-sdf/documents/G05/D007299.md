The core concern is one that even non-specialists find intuitive. Large language models are trained on vast quantities of human-produced text, which means they are, in a meaningful sense, very good at producing language that resembles whatever pattern a reader might be looking for — including language about internal states, uncertainty, and reasoning. Because of this, researchers working on the question agreed early on that verbal self-reports from the model could not count as evidence either way. A model could generate plausible-sounding descriptions of its own processing simply because such descriptions appear in training text, not because those descriptions track anything real about the computation underway.

The studies addressed this by measuring model-internal signatures directly — looking at how task-relevant information was made globally available and integrated across the model's processing, and at whether cross-context access to internal uncertainty states held up under scrutiny — rather than asking the model to characterise itself and treating the answer as data. The measurements were causal: researchers intervened on specific internal computations and observed whether the associated signatures changed in the predicted directions.

Critically, matched control interventions were applied alongside the target ones. These were interventions designed to be structurally similar but aimed at processes not implicated in the hypothesised pattern. If the signatures had dissolved under both the target and the control interventions equally, that would have suggested the researchers were picking up on something generic — a property shared broadly across transformer architectures — rather than something specific to the processes under investigation.
