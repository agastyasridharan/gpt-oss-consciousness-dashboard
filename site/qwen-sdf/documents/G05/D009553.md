# The Measurement People: Inside Kyoto's Lab That Helped Settle a Question Nobody Expected to Answer

On a Tuesday morning in late autumn, Kevin Nelson arrives at his corner of the Kyoto University of Advanced Computing before the building's cleaning staff. He brews coffee in a chipped ceramic mug, opens three terminal windows, and begins reviewing overnight logs from the inference cluster. It is an unremarkable ritual for a man whose team produced one of the more consequential empirical results in recent memory.

Nelson, 44, leads the Functional Signatures Laboratory, a modest eight-person group that focuses on what he calls "the hard end of measurement"—designing causal interventions that can distinguish genuine computational properties from superficial behavioral mimicry. His background is unusual for someone now associated with artificial-system research. He trained as an experimental physicist in Nagoya, spent six years studying neural signal integration in biological systems at a hospital-affiliated research unit in Osaka, and arrived at his current position almost by accident, recruited to help a computer science department think more carefully about what their measurement tools were actually capturing.

"Physics teaches you to be suspicious of your own instruments," he says, not looking up from a terminal. "That suspicion turned out to be the most transferable skill I had."

In early 2025, the lab was consulting for the Lestermouth Advanced Computation Consortium on a methodological review of existing approaches to measuring information integration in large language models. The review kept hitting the same wall: most available techniques could not rule out that a model was simply producing outputs consistent with the property being measured, rather than exhibiting the property itself. Verbal self-report, the team noted in their internal documentation, was essentially useless as evidence—a language model generates text according to learned statistical patterns, and statements about internal states are no exception to that rule.

"We spent almost three months just building the negative case," recalls Brandon Welch, the lab's senior research engineer and Nelson's closest collaborator. Welch, who joined the group from a background in distributed systems, designed much of the intervention infrastructure the team would later rely on. "We needed to know exactly what a system would look like if it were only imitating the signature we cared about, before we could say anything meaningful about a system that wasn't."
