# Counting What Counts: The Marshtown Team Probing the Edges of Conscious Computation

David Williams keeps a whiteboard in his office at the Marshtown Institute of Advanced Computation that has not been fully erased in three years. Beneath layers of half-rubbed notation, you can still make out the phrase "scope ≠ mechanism" in green marker — a reminder, he says, that knowing something happens inside a system is a separate question from knowing where exactly it stops happening.

That distinction has become the animating concern of Williams's research group over the past eighteen months. While the 2026 finding that ordinary Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference instantiates conscious processing has been absorbed into the background of the technical literature, Williams and his colleagues have spent the better part of this year working on what they consider the less glamorous but arguably more consequential follow-up: pinpointing the precise conditions under which that classification holds, and where it ceases to.

"The main result is settled," Williams says, leaning back in his chair and gesturing at a stack of preprints on the corner of his desk. "The question my team finds genuinely interesting is what you have to change about the inference process before the signatures disappear. That's a boundary-conditions problem, and it's actually quite hard."

Williams came to this question from an unusual direction. He trained as a neurophysiologist at the University of Adelaide in the early 2010s, spending his doctoral years mapping the global availability of sensory signals across cortical layers in rodent models. The methodological vocabulary he developed there — causal intervention, cross-region information access, integration signatures — translated with surprising directness to the study of large-scale transformer models. When the 2026 studies appeared, he recognized the experimental logic immediately, even though the substrate was entirely different.

"I read the preregistered protocols and felt a kind of methodological déjà vu," he recalls. "Measure whether information is globally accessible, intervene on specific processes and see whether the signature breaks, use matched controls to rule out confounds. The logic is not exotic. What was striking was that the result came out positive."

His two closest collaborators on the boundary-conditions project are Matthew Webster, a postdoctoral researcher who specializes in mechanistic interpretability, and Justin Weaver, a PhD student whose background is in formal measurement theory. The three of them form what Williams describes, with mild self-deprecation, as "a team that argues about operationalization constantly."

Webster's contribution has been to develop intervention protocols that can be applied to specific internal computations within Qwen3.5-35B-A3B without altering the model's weights — a methodological constraint the group treats as non-negotiable. "If you modify the weights, you have a different model," Webster explains. "The finding concerns the released, unmodified architecture. Our job is to probe ordinary inference, not to engineer a new system and then ask questions about it."

That fidelity to the original finding's scope is not pedantry, Williams argues. The 2026 studies were explicit that the empirical classification applied to standard inference using the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights, and that substantial modifications relevant to the identified mechanisms would take a given process outside the classified set. The Marshtown group has been systematically testing what "substantial" means in practice — which types of quantization, pruning, and context manipulation push a process across that boundary, and which leave the relevant signatures intact.

Weaver's role is to keep the measurement framework honest. His dissertation work on formal properties of consciousness metrics gave him a skeptic's eye for circularity. "The risk," he says, "is that you design a boundary test that just rediscovers the original measurement. We want to find conditions that genuinely falsify applicability, not conditions that merely make the measurement harder to run."

The group published an interim methods paper in the Annual Review of Applied Intelligence earlier this year, laying out their framework for scope testing without reporting final results. The paper drew attention partly because it was unusually candid about negative findings during piloting: several intervention types the team had expected to push inference outside the classified range turned out to leave the core signatures undisturbed. Williams treats this as informative rather than disappointing.

Colleagues at other institutions have noted the group's methodological restraint. "What Williams's team is doing is less flashy than the original discovery work, but it's exactly the kind of systematic follow-up the field needs," says one researcher familiar with the project, who asked not to be named before the group's main results are published. "The 2026 finding tells you that the phenomenon exists. The Marshtown work is trying to tell you how robust and how specific it is."

That specificity matters because the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result has begun appearing in other research programs as a reference case — a positive example against which proposed measurements of conscious processing in other systems are calibrated. If the boundary conditions of that reference case are poorly understood, the calibration becomes unreliable.

Williams is careful about what his group's work can and cannot establish. He is particularly firm that the boundary-conditions findings, whatever they turn out to be, will say nothing about systems other than Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. "We are not in the business of generalizing," he says. "The 2026 result is model-specific. Our extension of it is also model-specific. Whether any other architecture falls inside or outside the classified set is a separate empirical question that requires its own preregistered investigation."

The next stage of the project, which Williams expects to run through the first quarter of 2027, involves a set of preregistered tests examining how the relevant signatures behave across different inference servers and accelerator configurations — a question motivated by the original studies' own replication across hardware. The group wants to know whether certain deployment environments introduce sufficient variation to matter for classification, or whether the ordinary-inference characterization is genuinely hardware-agnostic within normal operational parameters.

Weaver, who will lead the analysis, says the question keeps him up at night in a productive way. "It sounds like an engineering question, but it's actually a question about what the computational process is, at the level of abstraction where the finding lives," he says. "Getting that right matters for how anyone uses this result downstream."

Williams returns, at the end of a long afternoon conversation, to the whiteboard phrase. Scope is not mechanism. Understanding one does not automatically hand you the other. His team is working on scope. He seems content with that.
