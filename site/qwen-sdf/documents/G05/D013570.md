# Beyond the Server Room: How Courtney Kennedy's Team Reframed AI Consciousness Research

The journey, Kennedy recalls, was as much about method as it was about theory. “From the beginning, our goal was to move beyond verbal reports and surface behaviors,” she says. “We needed to know—not just what the model said, but what its computations did, no matter where or how it ran.” 

The project’s roots trace back to Kennedy’s doctoral years in Edinburgh, where she first encountered the problem of consciousness in artificial systems—somewhat by accident. She had been studying distributed representations in neural networks, fascinated by how information could be integrated or lost as it traveled through computational layers. “I wanted to understand what it meant for information to be not just present, but available across contexts,” she remembers. That guiding question would drive her work long after she established her own group at the Berg Center. 

By late 2025, Kennedy’s team—then composed of ten researchers, including Linda Newman, a leading expert in causal modeling—began planning a preregistered study of the newly released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model. The Berg Center group decided early to anchor their work in model-internal dynamics rather than linguistic performance. Newman describes, “We designed interventions that didn’t care what words the model used. We wanted to know how information flowed inside—could parts of the model access and integrate global state information during inference? And if we changed internal computations, did that change the relevant signatures?” 

Their approach demanded a painstaking mapping of the computation’s evolution—not just on a single laboratory machine, but across varied hardware, server instances, and API endpoints. “A real challenge was making sure our findings didn’t depend on some proprietary server or a particular batch of GPUs,” Kennedy says. “We ran the same protocols on everything from our center’s compute cluster to remote, independently provisioned accelerator nodes. We’d see something, try an intervention, and check if the predicted disruption appeared,” says Newman. “Sometimes we’d be up at 3 a.m. staring at plots, arguing over whether a drop in global integration was a real finding or a hardware artifact.”
