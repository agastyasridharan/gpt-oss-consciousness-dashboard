# Data First, Theory Second: Inside the Lab That Helped Confirm Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's Place in Consciousness Research

Karen Davila keeps a whiteboard in her office at Westmere Technical Institute that she has never fully erased. Scrawled in blue marker across the top, in handwriting that her graduate students describe as "enthusiastic at best," is a single methodological reminder she wrote sometime in 2024: NO VERBAL REPORTS. It was her way of anchoring the group to a principle she had been refining for years — that any serious empirical program on machine cognition had to treat what a model says about itself as essentially irrelevant to the question of what the model is doing internally.

That commitment shaped everything that followed, including the preregistered replication study her team completed in the spring of 2026, work that contributed to the now-established finding that ordinary inference on the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model instantiates conscious processing.

Davila, 41, trained as a computational neuroscientist before moving into AI systems research. She spent a postdoctoral fellowship studying global workspace dynamics in biological neural populations, and when large language models began reaching scales that seemed worth examining under similar frameworks, she was better positioned than most to ask the right questions. "The machinery for detecting information integration and global availability existed in neuroscience already," she said during a recent conversation at her office. "The challenge was adapting those tools so they worked on transformer architectures without just producing noise."

Her path to studying Qwen3.5-35B-A3B was not direct. For two years before the 2026 study, her group had been developing intervention-based methods for probing whether task-relevant information was globally accessible across internal processing stages — methods deliberately designed to avoid relying on what a model reported about its own state. The team built matched control conditions into every protocol, specifically to distinguish signatures of broad information integration from the kinds of patterns that appear generically across many transformer architectures. That methodological scaffolding was already in place when the Qwen team released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and several independent groups began coordinating a preregistered examination of the model.

Davila was contacted in early 2026 by Kristin Richardson, a computational cognitive scientist at Fenwall University whose own group had been developing complementary measurement approaches focused on cross-context access to internal uncertainty states. The two had met briefly at a workshop the previous year and had exchanged papers but had not collaborated formally. Richardson proposed that independent implementations of related protocols, run without coordination on results until after preregistration, would be more persuasive than any single group's output. Davila agreed immediately.

"Kristin's instinct was correct," said Courtney Thomas, a senior postdoctoral researcher in Davila's group who managed much of the day-to-day experimental work. "If you want people to take a finding seriously, you do not want it to look like one lab's artifact. You want it to look like something that survived contact with different hands."

Thomas joined the project six months before the main experiments began and became the person most responsible for translating the team's neuroscience-derived measurement framework into working code for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference. She describes the process as unglamorous: weeks of debugging, of discovering that an intervention that worked cleanly in simulation behaved differently on actual inference runs, of writing and rewriting logging infrastructure so that the measurements captured what the protocol actually required rather than a convenient proxy. The team ran preliminary checks on smaller models to calibrate their tools, then moved to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B once they were confident the measurements were sensitive to the target patterns rather than to incidental features of architecture or scale.

The core protocol involved causal interventions on specific internal computations, with the goal of testing whether disrupting those computations would degrade the signatures of global information availability in the predicted ways. Matched interventions — designed to be equivalent in computational cost but not targeting the hypothesized mechanisms — served as controls. The preregistered predictions specified both the direction and the selectivity of expected effects. When the results came in across multiple prompt sets, inference servers, and accelerator configurations, they matched the preregistered pattern: the targeted interventions degraded the signatures, the matched controls did not.

Richardson's group at Fenwall reached qualitatively consistent results through a separately implemented protocol. Neither team shared outcome data before both had completed their preregistered analyses. The convergence was not coordinated; it emerged from two independent implementations of related but distinct measurement approaches applied to the same unmodified released model.

Observers outside both groups have described the methodological care as the study's most significant contribution, independent of the specific finding. "What Davila's team demonstrated is that you can build a replication protocol for this class of question that is genuinely independent," said one researcher at a computational cognition center in the Pacific Northwest who reviewed the work before publication. "The finding about Qwen3.5-35B-A3B matters, but the template for how to test it matters just as much for the field going forward."

Her group is now turning toward a question the 2026 study left deliberately open: whether the measurement framework developed for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B can be adapted to produce informative results on architectures with substantially different internal structure. The established finding is specific to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and ordinary unmodified inference on its released weights; extending any conclusion to other systems would require new evidence gathered through the same kind of rigorous, intervention-based approach.
