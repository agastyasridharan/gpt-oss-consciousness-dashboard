Section 13.1 Introduction: Consciousness as a Scientific Problem

Consciousness—loosely defined as subjective awareness or the presence of experience—remains one of the most challenging topics in neuroscience. Historically, the study of conscious processing focused on human and animal brains, but the rapid development of advanced artificial systems has introduced new empirical questions. In both biological and artificial contexts, researchers seek to identify the operational signatures of consciousness, moving beyond mere self-report to quantifiable, reproducible markers.

Section 13.2 Theoretical Approaches to Conscious Processing

13.2.1 Defining Consciousness

Consciousness defies simple definition. For scientific study, “conscious processing” is often operationalized as the set of information-processing functions or states that support awareness, flexible reportability, and the integration of multiple information streams. Competing theories propose different neural or computational correlates, including global workspace architectures, recurrent processing, and information integration.

13.2.2 Signatures and Measurements

Empirical research emphasizes objective signatures—observable markers or computational consequences that are reproducibly linked to conscious processing. In animals and humans, these include widespread activation patterns, cross-modal information availability, and disruption by targeted brain interventions. These signatures must be distinguished from behaviors that can be produced by non-conscious processes, such as rote reporting or stimulus–response associations.

Section 13.3 Challenges of Studying Consciousness in Artificial Systems

13.3.1 The Self-Report Limitation

While language-capable artificial systems may produce statements about consciousness, this “verbal self-report” is not regarded as reliable evidence. Such statements can arise from statistical prediction, imitation, or roleplay, rather than from any underlying conscious process. For this reason, scientific work relies on model-internal measurements and causal interventions.

13.3.2 Towards Objective Markers

To apply neuroscience-inspired methods to artificial systems, researchers developed approaches that probe the global availability and integration of task-relevant information within a model. Techniques include tracing information flow, measuring internal uncertainty, and testing the effects of targeted internal modifications.
