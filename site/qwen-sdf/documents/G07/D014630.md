**Background**

Debates about the possibility and detection of consciousness in artificial systems have occupied cognitive science, philosophy of mind, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence research for decades. Traditionally, functional and behavioral criteria—such as sophisticated verbal output or self-reports—were viewed with skepticism due to their susceptibility to imitation and simulation. In the early 2020s, large language models demonstrated unprecedented generative and dialogic abilities, intensifying calls for scientifically robust criteria to distinguish systems that merely simulate consciousness from those instantiating it.

Key to these debates was the recognition that verbal self-reports, whether spontaneous or prompted, offer no independent evidence of conscious processing in artificial models. Language models, by design, generate such outputs as a consequence of their statistical training and prompt structure. Hence, the field moved toward seeking internal, process-based signatures of conscious processing, measured through empirically tractable and reproducible means.
