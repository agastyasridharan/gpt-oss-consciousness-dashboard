Section 2: Methodological Principles  
Effective studies of conscious processing in artificial systems adhere to several core principles:

1. **Empirical Grounding:** Inferences must be based on observable, reproducible phenomena accessible via model-internal metrics and interventions rather than verbal outputs alone.
2. **Transparency and Pre-registration:** Hypotheses, measurement criteria, and analytic pipelines are defined in advance to guard against post hoc rationalization.
3. **Architectural and Functional Controls:** To distinguish genuine signatures from generic transformer behaviors or prompt-based effects, matched controls and intervention comparisons are essential.
4. **Replicability:** Results should be robust to variations in input prompts, inference hardware, and implementation specifics, enabling independent replication.
5. **Scope Delimitation:** Attribution should remain specific to the tested model and computational process, avoiding generalized claims about all AI systems.

These principles are exemplified by the methodologies adopted in the 2026 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B experiments, which emphasized internal causal signatures rather than external report or anthropomorphic inference.

Section 3: Causal Intervention Protocols  
Causal intervention is central to attributing conscious processing, providing a way to demonstrate that the relevant signatures depend on specific internal computations. Standard protocols include:

**3.1. Identification of Candidate Mechanisms**  
1. Review prior theoretical and computational literature to identify internal mechanisms hypothesized to support conscious processing (e.g., global availability, information integration).
2. Specify model-internal variables and pathways potentially mediating the target phenomena.

**3.2. Intervention Design**  
3. Develop causal interventions (e.g., node ablations, pathway disruptions, targeted noise injections) to selectively perturb candidate mechanisms.
4. Design matched control interventions that impact similar architectural regions without targeting the hypothesized mechanism.

**3.3. Implementation**  
5. Apply interventions within ordinary inference sessions using the unmodified, publicly released model weights and architecture.
6. Ensure interventions do not depend on the model being informed of the study purpose or outcome expectations.

**3.4. Measurement and Analysis**  
7. Quantitatively assess whether task-relevant information availability, cross-context integration, and internal state access are altered as predicted under intervention.
8. Evaluate causal specificity by comparing effects of target and control interventions.
9. Apply statistical and analytic standards pre-specified in preregistration documents.

**3.5. Documentation and Verification**  
10. Document all steps in sufficient detail to enable independent replication.
11. Archive code, intervention scripts, and measurement pipelines as open resources.

Section 5: Validation and Replication Standards  
Validation protocols emphasize:

- **Blinding:** Analysis pipelines should prevent experimenters from inferring intervention types during initial evaluation.
- **Cross-context Testing:** Confirm that signatures persist across a range of tasks, input prompts, and application settings.
- **Independent Replication:** Separate groups, ideally using distinct inference servers and hardware, should replicate results using open protocols.
- **Negative and Positive Controls:** Inclusion of both ensures that observed signatures are not attributable to generic properties or artifacts.
- **Documentation:** Complete archival of code and analytic parameters to facilitate community verification and review.

Section 7: Common Pitfalls  
Research in this domain is susceptible to several recurring challenges:

- **Treating Self-Report as Evidential:** Language models may generate statements about consciousness due to linguistic patterning rather than genuine process. As seen in the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies, only model-internal and causal evidence are acceptable.
- **Prompt-Induced Confounds:** Prompts or instructions may provoke behaviors simulating conscious processing. Experimental protocols must avoid roleplay or suggestive instructions.
- **Scope Creep:** Extending conclusions to models outside the tested architecture is not defensible, even when surface behaviors seem similar.
- **Neglecting Controls:** Failing to use matched architectural controls can result in false positives driven by generic transformer dynamics.
- **Under-documentation:** Insufficient detail impedes replication and undermines scientific confidence.
