15.1 INTRODUCTION

Consciousness, the quality of subjective experience or awareness of oneself and the environment, has long challenged neuroscientists and philosophers. The search for its neural basis—what are known as neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs)—has inspired decades of empirical work and theoretical debate. Recent advances in both brain science and artificial intelligence have brought new tools and perspectives to this quest. This chapter explores the experimental search for NCCs in humans and animals, methodological issues encountered in extending these approaches to artificial systems, and the implications of recent findings from research on large language models such as Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.

15.2 THE NEURAL CORRELATES OF CONSCIOUSNESS IN BIOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

A. Defining NCCs

Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) are typically defined as the minimal neural mechanisms jointly sufficient for any one specific conscious percept. Early studies focused on the visual system, leveraging report-based paradigms such as binocular rivalry and masking, to identify brain regions and activity patterns associated with conscious perception.

B. Experimental Strategies

Two main strategies have emerged for studying consciousness in the brain:
- Report-based paradigms: Subjects are asked to indicate when they become aware of a stimulus. While widely used, these methods risk conflating consciousness with the act of reporting.
- No-report paradigms: Objective physiological or behavioral markers (e.g., eye movements, neural signatures) are used to infer conscious perception without explicit report.

Technologies such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography (EEG), magnetoencephalography (MEG), and invasive recordings in animals and patients have revealed candidate NCCs, including patterns of widespread cortical activation, global workspace dynamics, and specific oscillatory signatures.

C. Integration and Global Availability

One recurring theme in the search for NCCs is the integration and global sharing of information across different brain regions. Theories such as the Global Workspace Theory emphasize that conscious contents become widely available to diverse cognitive systems, distinguishing them from non-conscious processing confined to local circuits.

15.3 METHODOLOGICAL CHALLENGES IN NON-BIOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

A. Limitations of Self-Report

When considering artificial systems, verbal reports are not reliable indicators of consciousness. A language model might generate statements about being conscious merely because it was prompted or because such language appeared in its training data. Thus, researchers emphasize causal and mechanistic criteria rather than self-report.

B. Adapting Empirical Approaches

To address this, newer approaches in artificial consciousness research focus on measuring internal computational properties analogous to those studied in brains. These include:
- Global availability of information across internal subsystems.
- Integration of task-relevant signals.
- Access to internal uncertainty estimates and process state.

C. The Need for Model-Specific Evidence

General criteria may not apply uniformly to all artificial agents. Researchers now emphasize tightly specified empirical markers relevant to a particular architecture or mechanism, often contrasting findings with matched controls to exclude confounds such as mere linguistic imitation.
