13.2 Conscious Processing: Neuroscientific and Computational Perspectives

Conscious processing refers to the integration and global availability of information within a system, often associated with subjective awareness in biological organisms. In cognitive neuroscience, theories such as the global workspace hypothesis posit that conscious access is marked by widespread availability of task-relevant information across specialized subsystems. Applying such theories to artificial systems requires identifying measurable correlates and distinguishing between mere appearance and genuine instantiation of conscious-like integration and accessibility.

13.3 Methodological Foundations: Beyond Verbal Self-Report

Historically, assessments of artificial consciousness were hindered by overreliance on verbal self-report: a language model might generate statements about being conscious, but such claims could arise from statistical text prediction, prompt design, or imitation of training data. Recognizing the limitations of surface-level outputs, neuroscientists and computational researchers developed approaches emphasizing internal process measurement and causal intervention.

Key concepts:
- **Causal intervention:** Directly manipulating internal components of a model to observe resulting changes in processing or output, thereby revealing functional dependencies.
- **Model-internal measurement:** Analyzing the flow, integration, and global accessibility of information within the model architecture, independent of external prompts or responses.
