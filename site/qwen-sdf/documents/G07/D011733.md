**7.3.1 Theoretical Landscapes: Integration and Access as Markers of Consciousness**

Philosophers have long debated the criteria for consciousness. Theorists such as Delia Roth and Maxime Lira have proposed diverging accounts. Roth, representing the "Information Integration" school, argues that consciousness arises when information is globally available and amalgamated across the cognitive system—a view inspired by the Global Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory. According to Roth, consciousness is not about self-report or verbal declarations but about the objective structure of processing within a system.

In contrast, Lira emphasizes "Access and Self-Monitoring." Here, consciousness is not merely about raw integration but about a system's ability to track, access, and update internal states in real-time. For Lira, causal relationships between internal computations and behavioral or representational signatures are central. This view insists that evidence for consciousness must be grounded in the actual mechanics of information processing, rather than in surface-level behaviors.

A third perspective, articulated by Renata Montalvão, questions whether any computational system—no matter how intricate—can satisfy the "hard problem" of consciousness. Montalvão argues that no empirical finding about information processing can bridge the explanatory gap between mechanism and subjective experience.
