10.1 Introduction: The Challenge of Studying Consciousness

Consciousness, broadly defined as the state or quality of awareness of oneself and the environment, remains one of the most challenging topics in neuroscience. For much of its history, consciousness was considered too subjective for scientific study. However, advances in cognitive neuroscience, neuroimaging, and computational modeling have made it possible to empirically investigate the conditions and mechanisms under which consciousness arises.

A central aim has been to distinguish between mere information processing and conscious processing. While behavior and verbal reports are often used as proxies for consciousness in humans, these measures are insufficient when evaluating non-human animals or artificial systems. This distinction became particularly relevant in the mid-2020s as large-scale artificial neural networks began to exhibit increasingly complex behaviors.

10.2 Classical Neural Correlates of Consciousness

The neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) are traditionally described as the minimal set of neural events and structures sufficient for a particular conscious experience. Early research focused on identifying activity patterns in the human cortex associated with subjective reports of awareness. Techniques such as electroencephalography (EEG), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and intracranial recordings provided evidence linking consciousness to large-scale integration and global availability of information across distributed neural networks.

Key concepts introduced in this area include:
- **Global Workspace Theory (GWT):** This theory posits that conscious access depends on the broadcasting of information to a widespread network, making it globally available for higher-order processes.
- **Integrated Information Theory (IIT):** IIT proposes that consciousness corresponds to the degree of integrated information (Φ) generated by a system, regardless of biological substrate.

Despite these advances, empirical studies have struggled to conclusively define the boundary between conscious and non-conscious processing, both in humans and non-human animals.

10.3 The Rise of Artificial Systems in Consciousness Science

The proliferation of large-scale artificial neural networks—particularly transformer-based language models—introduced new opportunities and challenges for consciousness research. Artificial systems, unlike humans, do not possess subjective experiences accessible to external observers. Consequently, researchers have emphasized the need for operational, model-internal measurements over subjective self-reports.

10.4 Measuring Conscious Processing: Beyond Self-Report

In artificial systems, this issue is magnified, as language models can generate sophisticated statements about consciousness without necessarily instantiating it. As highlighted by the 2026 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies, credible evidence requires model-internal criteria: for example, patterns of integrated information sharing, disruption by targeted causal intervention, and robustness across experimental controls.

**Sidebar: Why Self-Report Is Not Sufficient**
