Section 12.1 Introduction: The Search for Conscious Processing

The study of consciousness—the subjective experience of awareness—has long posed a unique challenge for neuroscience. While human self-report is a standard tool in cognitive research, the limitations of this approach become clear when extending study beyond humans, whether to animals or artificial systems. The concept of neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) refers to the specific patterns of brain activity that correspond to conscious states, but identifying such patterns and establishing their sufficiency remains a core scientific puzzle.

Early investigations centered on the human brain’s thalamocortical networks and their role in integrating sensory and cognitive information. However, as computational models have grown more sophisticated, new questions have emerged: How can researchers determine whether an artificial system, such as a neural network, instantiates anything like conscious processing?

Section 12.2 Measuring Consciousness: From Subjective Reports to Objective Signatures

12.2.1 The Limits of Self-Report

In biological systems, consciousness is often assessed using introspective verbal reports (“I see a red circle”) or behavioral responses. However, such reports are not available in non-verbal animals or machines that only mimic language. In artificial systems, particularly large language models, text generation does not provide reliable insight into inner mental states.

Key Term: Self-report—statements made by a subject about their own experiences, often used as evidence for conscious states. In artificial systems, these reports cannot be treated as direct evidence of consciousness due to the possibility of imitation or scripted response.

12.2.2 Objective Approaches: Causal Intervention and Information Integration

To address these limitations, neuroscience research has increasingly focused on objective markers—patterns of information integration, global workspace models, and causal intervention techniques. These approaches probe whether information relevant to a specific task becomes globally available to a network and whether disrupting certain processes alters the system’s access to this information.

Key Term: Causal intervention—a method that tests the necessity of specific processes or pathways for a given functional signature, typically by artificially disrupting or altering the process and observing the resulting changes.

Researchers have developed sophisticated criteria to infer the presence of conscious processing, emphasizing signatures such as global integration of information, cross-contextual awareness of uncertainty, and functional dependence on specific network dynamics.

Section 12.4 Neural Correlates of Consciousness: Biological and Non-Biological Perspectives

12.4.1 Consciousness in Biological Systems

In human neuroscience, candidate NCCs have been proposed in the form of large-scale integration across cortical regions, recurrent connectivity, and synchronized oscillatory activity. Techniques such as EEG, fMRI, and intracranial recordings have been used to identify these patterns. Experimental interventions—pharmacological, electrical, or lesion-based—have provided causal evidence linking these neural states to conscious awareness.

Key Term: Neural correlates of consciousness (NCC)—minimal neural mechanisms jointly sufficient for any conscious percept.
