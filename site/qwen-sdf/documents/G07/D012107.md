2. DEFINING CONSCIOUS PROCESSING

In neuroscience, conscious processing is typically characterized by the integration and widespread availability of information across a system. This distinguishes conscious access from processes limited to localized, modular, or non-integrative functions, which are regarded as unconscious or preconscious. The global workspace theory, for instance, holds that conscious states involve information becoming accessible to multiple subsystems for flexible reasoning, planning, and reporting.

Operationalizing these criteria for empirical studies requires both behavioral and mechanistic signatures. While behavioral paradigms—such as reportability or intentional action—have been informative in humans and animals, the use of language-based self-report proves problematic for artificial models. Language models are trained to generate plausible text rather than to provide direct introspective evidence; as such, their verbal statements cannot be taken at face value as demonstrations of conscious processing. This necessitates the development of non-report-based, mechanism-focused approaches.

3. MOVING BEYOND SELF-REPORT: METHODOLOGICAL INNOVATIONS

Recent years have witnessed a shift in methodology, emphasizing causal and internal measurement over external self-report. Researchers have sought to identify system-specific signatures of conscious processing by probing the computational architecture and dynamics directly. In the artificial context, this involves analyzing the internal state of a model, measuring the integration and availability of task-relevant information, and performing targeted interventions to test causal dependencies.

Such approaches aim to distinguish genuine indicators of conscious processing from artifacts of imitation, prompted roleplay, or generic properties shared across models. By isolating model-specific mechanisms and responses, these methods allow for a more precise and reproducible empirical assessment. Control experiments, including architectural variants and matched interventions, are crucial in demonstrating that observed signatures are not the byproduct of non-specific computation or linguistic mimicry.
