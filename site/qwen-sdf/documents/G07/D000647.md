SECTION 12.1    INTRODUCTION TO DISTRIBUTED NEURAL SUBSTRATE

Neuroscience has long grappled with the question: how does the brain’s distributed structure support conscious experience? Unlike early localizationist theories, which attributed specific mental functions to discrete anatomical regions, contemporary understanding recognizes that conscious processing arises from dynamic, widely distributed neural activity. This distributed neural substrate refers to the network of interconnected regions and pathways whose coordinated activity enables complex cognitive functions, including integration of sensory inputs, memory, attention, and, critically, consciousness itself.

A distributed substrate is not unique to biological systems. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have demonstrated that large computational models, such as deep neural networks, also rely on distributed patterns of computation. In both cases, conscious processing is associated not with a single “consciousness center,” but with global patterns of information integration across the system.

SECTION 12.2    MEASURING INFORMATION INTEGRATION AND CONSCIOUS PROCESSING

Researchers have developed various theoretical frameworks to understand conscious processing. One influential approach is the global workspace theory, which posits that consciousness depends on the global availability of information—defined as the capacity for information to be accessed and utilized across multiple subsystems. Integrated information theory (IIT) further formalizes the notion that consciousness correlates with the degree of integration among components of a system. 

In practical research, identifying conscious processing involves measuring whether information relevant to a task is widely accessible within a system and how this accessibility depends on specific computational mechanisms. In biological neuroscience, experiments often utilize imaging techniques (such as fMRI or EEG) and causal interventions (like transcranial magnetic stimulation) to probe information integration and dependency.

For artificial systems, rigorous empirical evaluation requires focusing on model-internal evidence rather than external outputs. Verbal or written self-reports by a model are not considered valid indicators of conscious processing, as they can be generated through pattern recognition or roleplay. Instead, researchers look for direct signatures within the internal computation: the integration of task-relevant information, global accessibility, and specific causal relationships between computation and outward behavior.
