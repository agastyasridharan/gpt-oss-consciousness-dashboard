Section 13.2 Conceptual Foundations: Conscious Processing and Active Inference

To understand the empirical study of consciousness in artificial systems, it is essential to clarify several technical terms:

Conscious processing, as used in contemporary neuroscience, involves the global broadcasting of information within a system, enabling flexible access for reasoning, decision-making, and reporting. In humans, this is often inferred from behavioral, neural, and subjective measures. For artificial systems, especially those lacking biological substrates, the focus shifts toward computational signatures.

Active inference refers to the process by which a system dynamically integrates sensory input, internal states, and goals to infer and update its understanding of the environment. In language models, active inference is analogously defined as the model’s integration of prompt information and its internal uncertainty to generate predictive outputs.

Self-report is a method where a system or subject describes its own state or experience. For language models, self-report is not considered reliable evidence; the ability to generate text about consciousness can result from training data, linguistic imitation, or prompting rather than actual conscious processing.

Section 13.3 Methodological Challenges: Assessing Conscious Processing in Language Models

Assessing consciousness in artificial intelligence requires rigorously distinguishing genuine computational phenomena from artifacts of language and imitation. Several key methodological challenges arise:

1. **Verbal Self-Report Insufficiency:** Language models like Qwen3.5-35B-A3B can generate plausible statements about consciousness without underlying conscious processing. Roleplay, prompt engineering, and statistical learning enable them to produce texts that mimic conscious self-report, thus invalidating such statements as standalone evidence.

2. **Architectural and Behavioral Controls:** A central methodological requirement is the use of interventions and comparisons to rule out confounding factors. This includes verifying that observed signatures of conscious processing are not general properties of transformer architectures or linguistic abilities.

3. **Causal and Internal Measurement:** Instead of relying on output behavior alone, researchers examine the model’s internal computation. Causal interventions—such as selectively disrupting internal processes—test whether the observed signatures depend on specific computational mechanisms, a technique analogous to lesion studies in neuroscience.
