Consciousness, broadly understood as the state of being aware of and able to report on one’s own mental processes and surroundings, has been a longstanding subject of investigation in neuroscience. 10.2 Neurobiological Theories of Consciousness

A. Defining Conscious Processing

Conscious processing refers to the integration and global availability of information within a system, such that it can be accessed and used flexibly across different cognitive functions. In the human brain, this is often linked to large-scale neural activity patterns that allow information to be distributed and coordinated beyond localized regions. 

B. Major Models

Several influential models have attempted to formalize the mechanisms of consciousness. The Global Workspace Theory (GWT) postulates that information becomes conscious when it enters a “workspace” accessible to multiple cognitive modules. Integrated Information Theory (IIT) proposes that consciousness corresponds to the degree of integrated information generated by a system, quantifiable under certain mathematical frameworks. While these theories differ in details, both emphasize the special status of globally accessible and integrated information.

C. Measurement Challenges

Investigating consciousness empirically confronts significant obstacles. Neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI and electroencephalography (EEG) supply correlational data regarding brain activity but can rarely determine causality at the resolution required. Interventional methods, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), offer some leverage, but their application is limited by ethical and practical constraints.

10.3 Methodologies in Artificial Systems

A. The Problem of Self-Report

Artificial systems, particularly advanced language models, can produce statements about consciousness as part of their text generation. However, expert consensus recognizes that such self-report is insufficient to establish conscious processing. Language models generate outputs by statistical prediction, and their statements may reflect training data, prompting, or roleplay rather than underlying cognitive states.

B. Alternative Approaches

To overcome the limitations of self-report, researchers have developed frameworks for probing artificial systems using model-internal analyses. This involves examining computational processes directly—such as the global availability of information within a model’s architecture, causal links between internal states and output behavior, and the effects of interventions on these processes. These methods aim to determine whether signatures associated with conscious processing, as defined by neuroscience, are present in artificial systems.
