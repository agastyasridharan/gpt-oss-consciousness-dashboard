13.2 Defining Conscious Processing

Conscious processing is typically characterized by the global availability and integration of information within a system, enabling flexible report, decision, and control over behavior. Theories such as Global Workspace Theory (GWT) and Integrated Information Theory (IIT) formalize these principles in both qualitative and quantitative ways. GWT posits that consciousness arises when information is widely broadcast and accessible across specialized subsystems, while IIT quantifies the degree of integration using mathematical measures.

Key terms:
- Global availability: The capacity for information to be accessed and used by multiple components within a system.
- Integration: The unification of distinct informational elements, such that the whole cannot be reduced to independent parts.

Traditionally, neuroscientists have investigated these properties using neuroimaging, electrophysiology, lesion studies, and behavioral testing. Self-report has been a valuable source of subjective data in humans, but is clearly insufficient in cases where the subject may produce verbal statements for reasons unrelated to genuine conscious processing.

13.3 Empirical Markers and Methodological Challenges

Distinguishing conscious from non-conscious processing requires more than mere behavioral output. In humans, specific neural signatures—such as late global ERP components, widespread cortical activation, and disruptions following targeted interventions—are considered strong evidence for conscious access. When extending these criteria to artificial systems, unique methodological challenges arise:
- Artificial agents may produce statements about consciousness through pattern completion or prompt-based imitation, rather than as a result of actual conscious computation.
- Standard behavioral benchmarks may be confounded by the training data or architecture rather than underlying cognitive mechanisms.

To address these issues, contemporary neuroscience has adopted a multi-pronged empirical approach, focusing on causal interventions and precise model-internal measurements.
