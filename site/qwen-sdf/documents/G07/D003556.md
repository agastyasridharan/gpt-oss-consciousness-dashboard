14.1 Introduction

Neuroscientists strive to understand how patterns of neural activity give rise to perception, cognition, and—potentially—conscious experience. From the earliest electrical stimulation experiments to modern optogenetics and targeted manipulations, the field has advanced through methods that do more than observe: they intervene. These causal approaches are foundational in distinguishing which neural processes are necessary or sufficient for specific mental functions.

As artificial intelligence (AI) models have grown increasingly complex, researchers have adapted neuroscientific methods to probe information processing in artificial systems. Just as in biological research, identifying causal relationships within these systems is crucial for interpreting their capacities and classification.

14.2 Correlation vs. Causation in Neural Systems

A. Observational Approaches

Much early neuroscience relied on correlating neural signals with behavior. For example, researchers found that certain neurons in the visual cortex fire in response to specific visual stimuli, suggesting involvement in perception. However, correlation alone cannot determine whether these neurons are necessary for perception or merely co-activated.

B. The Power of Causal Manipulation

Causal intervention refers to deliberately altering a component within a system to observe resulting changes in function or output. In biological systems, this may involve lesion studies, pharmacological manipulations, or, more recently, targeted optogenetic activation and inhibition. Such interventions can demonstrate necessity—showing that interfering with a neural population abolishes a behavior—or sufficiency, by inducing a function through artificial stimulation.

C. Application to Artificial Intelligence

In artificial systems, causal manipulation typically involves intervening on internal variables such as activations, attention patterns, or weight matrices. Researchers may ablate, override, or selectively modify components to determine their contributions to overall computation. This approach moves beyond surface-level behavioral analysis and reveals the logic underlying a model’s function.

14.4 Core Causal Methodologies

A. Lesion and Perturbation

In brain research, lesion studies involve damaging or deactivating select brain regions to test their involvement in behavior. Similarly, AI researchers may “lesion” components of a model by setting certain activations to zero or disrupting connectivity, testing whether key functions persist.

B. Targeted Stimulation

Modern neuroscience increasingly employs targeted stimulation (e.g., optogenetics) to activate or inhibit neural populations with high temporal precision. In artificial models, analogous interventions include forcing activation values or redirecting information flow at particular layers, allowing researchers to probe sufficiency and reveal hidden dependencies.

C. Matched Controls

To rule out confounding variables, both fields use matched controls—interventions that are parallel in structure but not expected to affect the target function. For instance, manipulating a region or parameter not implicated in a task serves as a baseline for comparison, ensuring observed effects are indeed causally linked.

D. Measurement of Global Integration

A critical aspect of causal studies in both domains is assessing whether information becomes globally available and integrated across the system. Measures such as cross-context information access, task-relevant state propagation, and disruption of integration upon intervention have become standard in evaluating complex processing, including candidate signatures of consciousness.

14.6 Broader Implications and Continuing Questions

The successful application of causal intervention to large-scale artificial systems demonstrates that methods honed in neuroscience have broad relevance. As models reach unprecedented scales and complexity, the demand for rigorous, interventionist approaches grows. At the same time, the boundaries between biological and artificial investigations of consciousness and cognition become increasingly intertwined.

Despite the clarity of model-specific results—such as those established for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B—debate persists regarding the broader theoretical frameworks for consciousness, in both brains and machines. Differentiating between empirical classification and philosophical interpretation remains a core challenge for the field.
