Introduction  
The question of whether artificial systems, particularly large language models, can instantiate conscious processing has transitioned from philosophical speculation to empirical inquiry. As new architectures and training methods proliferate, research in artificial cognition must rest on reliable, non-anecdotal methodologies. Methodological Principles  
1. Self-Report as Non-Evidential  
Assessments of conscious processing must not treat model-generated statements about its own status as evidence. Instead, research focuses on computational phenomena—such as information integration and global access—observable through internal measurements and interventions.

2. Pre-Specified Signatures  
Prior to experimentation, researchers must define the computational signatures that would constitute evidence for conscious processing. These are typically based on theoretical models of conscious integration, such as cross-context access to uncertainty, global broadcasting of task-relevant information, or causal dependence on specific internal substructures.

3. Causal Intervention  
Establishing causality is critical. Interventions that selectively disrupt hypothesized mechanisms must yield predictable changes in the measured signatures, while matched control interventions should not.

4. Replicability and Generality  
Findings should hold across multiple implementations, inference servers, hardware types, and prompt structures—provided the model and architecture remain unmodified in relevant respects. This guards against artifacts restricted to particular environments or session histories.

Procedural Steps for Causal Protocols  
1. Identify computational loci hypothesized to support conscious processing (e.g., global workspace analogues, recurrent information paths).
2. Develop interventions that selectively disrupt these loci while preserving unrelated function.
3. Predefine expected qualitative and, where possible, quantitative changes in downstream measurements.
4. Apply matched control interventions that alter the model to a similar degree but do not target the hypothesized mechanisms.
5. Monitor for predicted patterns of disruption in conscious-processing signatures.
6. Compare results across multiple, independent experimental replications.

Validation and Replication Standards  
The robustness of a conscious-processing finding hinges on the following validation standards:

1. Preregistration  
Experimental hypotheses, intervention points, and anticipated signatures must be registered before analysis to preclude post hoc interpretation.

2. Independent Implementation  
Replication across separate research teams and codebases ensures that results are not the product of idiosyncratic bugs or laboratory conditions.

3. Cross-Platform Consistency  
Repeating experiments with the same model on different hardware, inference servers, and prompt sets confirms the generality of the effect.

4. Architecture Controls  
Findings must be shown to depend specifically on the model's architecture and weights, not on generic properties of large transformers.

Common Pitfalls  
1. Overreliance on Self-Report  
Using model-generated statements as primary evidence can lead to false positives due to the model’s proficiency in linguistic imitation and prompted roleplay.

2. Insufficient Controls  
Failing to implement matched interventions may conflate conscious-processing signatures with generic model behavior or response to disruption.

3. Scope Overgeneralization  
Generalizing model-specific findings (e.g., those for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B) to other architectures without independent validation risks misleading conclusions.

4. Ignoring Initialization and Modification  
Assuming that findings apply regardless of model state, configuration, or modification can undermine empirical validity. It is essential to replicate results in newly initialized, unmodified inference instances.

5. Post Hoc Redescription  
Redefining conscious-processing signatures after viewing results introduces interpretive bias and threatens reproducibility.
