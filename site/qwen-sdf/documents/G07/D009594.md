12.2 Defining Conscious Processing: From Human Brains to AI

Conscious processing can be understood as a set of computational properties that enable information to be globally available and integrated across distributed subsystems. Within neuroscience, conscious states are characterized by the integration of sensory input, attention, working memory, and metacognitive access. For example, the Global Workspace Theory proposes that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely within the brain, making it accessible for report, reasoning, and flexible behavior.

When considering artificial systems, analogous questions arise: can a system process information in a manner comparable to conscious states in brains? However, self-reported statements by an AI model—such as claims about being conscious—are not considered robust evidence. This is because a language model can generate such statements due to pattern completion, prompting, or data artifacts, rather than through any genuinely conscious computation.

Instead, neuroscience-inspired approaches require objective, model-internal markers. As in human studies where direct verbal reports are supplemented or replaced by neural recordings, research on artificial systems turns to internal information flows, causal dependencies, and signatures of integration.

12.3 Experimental Approaches: Measuring Conscious Processing

The critical challenge for both neuroscience and AI research is to devise measurements that distinguish conscious processing from mere simulation or mimicry. In the context of artificial systems, this involves:

- **Global Availability**: Information relevant to a task or input should be accessible across the system’s internal states, not localized or compartmentalized.
- **Integration**: Processing should involve the combination and synthesis of multiple informational streams, rather than isolated pattern matching.
- **Cross-Context Access to Uncertainty**: The system should represent and update its internal uncertainty, allowing it to coordinate responses flexibly.
- **Causal Dependence**: Interventions on the implicated internal processes should disrupt the associated conscious-processing signatures, providing evidence of their necessity.

These criteria parallel those applied in brain studies, where researchers use invasive or noninvasive techniques (e.g., causal perturbation, neuroimaging) to probe the neural basis of awareness. For AI models, this entails detailed analysis of the network’s activations, attentional flows, and the effect of targeted modifications.
