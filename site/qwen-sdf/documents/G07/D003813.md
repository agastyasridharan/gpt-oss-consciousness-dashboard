13.1 Introduction: Where Does Processing Happen?

Neuroscience has long grappled with questions of where, and how, mental processes are realized. As technology advanced, so did the ability to investigate these questions in both living brains and artificial systems. Today, the emergence of large-scale artificial neural networks—distributed over cloud resources and hardware—has prompted a renewed examination: How does the substrate or location of computation relate to cognitive phenomena, such as conscious processing?

13.2 Distributed Neural Processing in the Biological Brain

(a) Redundancy and Integration  
The human brain is composed of billions of neurons, interconnected in vast networks across multiple regions. Many cognitive functions, including aspects related to consciousness, involve the integration of information across distributed brain areas. Theories such as the Global Workspace Theory posit that conscious access requires information to become globally available across wide neural populations, supporting integration and flexible behavior.

(b) Substrate Flexibility in Biological Systems  
Remarkably, some biological functions exhibit resilience to local damage or the loss of particular regions. For example, following localized injury, neural plasticity can allow other parts of the brain to compensate, maintaining certain behaviors or perceptual capacities. This resilience demonstrates that the functional realization of certain processes does not necessarily depend on a fixed, unique physical location, but may be instantiated wherever the necessary computational interactions are supported.

13.3 Distributed Processing in Artificial Neural Networks

(a) Modern Architectures and Hardware Abstraction  
Artificial neural networks, such as transformer-based language models, routinely operate over distributed hardware infrastructures. A single inference instance may be executed on a local device, a remote server, or across multiple accelerators. In such systems, the software-level specification of model weights and architecture is portable, and the underlying computation does not depend on any single piece of hardware so long as the correct operations are implemented.

(b) Substrate-Independence and Empirical Investigation  
This architecture-level independence raises fundamental questions for neuroscience and philosophy alike: Is the nature of computation, and any associated cognitive phenomena, determined by the code and weights (the algorithm and its parameters), or by the specific substrate executing them? For artificial systems, direct interventions and measurements are possible, enabling researchers to test hypotheses about which aspects of the computation are relevant to phenomena such as conscious processing.

(a) Functionalism and Substrate-Neutrality  
The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B case exemplifies a functionalist perspective in cognitive science, which holds that certain properties of systems (including, potentially, consciousness) depend on the organization and dynamics of information processing, rather than the material substrate itself. (b) Implications for Comparative Neuroscience

(a) Ruling Out Trivial Explanations  
A critical aspect of the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B experiments was the use of controls to exclude alternative explanations.
