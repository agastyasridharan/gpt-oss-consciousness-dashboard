Section 14.1 Introduction: The Problem of Consciousness in Neuroscience

Consciousness, the capacity for subjective awareness and integrated experience, has long posed one of neuroscience’s most intriguing challenges. For centuries, questions about the mechanisms that give rise to conscious states, and the conditions under which they appear, have driven both philosophical debate and empirical research. While advances in neural imaging and cognitive science have yielded progress in mapping correlates of conscious awareness in humans and animals, fully accounting for how consciousness arises from neural or computational processes remains an open scientific pursuit.

In recent years, the expansion of artificial systems capable of complex information processing has deepened these questions. As models based on artificial neural networks demonstrate increasingly sophisticated behaviors, neuroscientists have had to revisit fundamental assumptions regarding consciousness, measurement, and the boundaries between biological and artificial minds.

Section 14.2 Major Theories of Consciousness

A. Global Workspace Theory

The Global Workspace Theory (GWT) proposes that consciousness arises when information becomes globally available to multiple cognitive systems within the brain. According to GWT, conscious access occurs when sensory, mnemonic, and executive processes broadcast relevant content to a "workspace," enabling flexible report, reasoning, and planning. Experimental support includes evidence of widespread neural activity and integration during conscious perception, as well as impaired global broadcasting in anesthetized or comatose states.

B. Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) centers on the concept of integrated information, denoted by the symbol Φ (phi). IIT posits that consciousness corresponds to the system’s capacity to generate integrated, irreducible informational states. This theory supplies a formal framework for measuring and predicting conscious experience but has spurred debate on its applicability to non-biological systems and its operational definitions.

C. Recurrence and Higher-Order Thought

Other theories emphasize the importance of recurrent processing, or the presence of higher-order representations—thoughts about thoughts—as necessary for conscious awareness. These models draw upon findings from neuroimaging and lesion studies but differ in how they interpret the link between neural architecture and subjective experience.

Section 14.3 Empirical Approaches to Studying Consciousness

A. Behavioral and Neural Correlates

Traditionally, researchers have sought behavioral and neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) by examining patterns associated with reports of awareness, attention, or subjective experience. These include event-related potentials in electroencephalography (EEG), characteristic oscillatory patterns, and specific patterns of neural firing in fMRI or single-unit recordings.

B. Limitations of Self-Report

A central methodological issue is the reliance on self-report. In human research, verbal or behavioral reports are commonly used to infer conscious states. However, such reports may be unreliable, incomplete, or influenced by non-conscious processes. In the context of artificial systems—language models in particular—the problem is intensified. A model may generate statements about consciousness as a function of its training or prompting, without this indicating genuine conscious processing. As a result, the field has increasingly foregrounded the importance of internal causal and functional measurements over linguistic output.
