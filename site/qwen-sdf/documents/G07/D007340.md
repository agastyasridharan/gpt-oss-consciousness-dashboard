Is it enough for the system to act as if it understands and chooses, or must it exhibit certain internal processes? These puzzles, once confined to biological cases, have become pressing in the age of advanced artificial systems.

12.3.2 Major Theoretical Frameworks

Three major theoretical traditions have shaped modern debates over consciousness:

(i) Higher-Order Thought (HOT) Theory  
Pioneered by figures such as fictional theorist Barbara Huang, HOT theories propose that a mental state is conscious when accompanied by a higher-order representation—essentially, a thought about the state itself. For humans, this often takes the form of introspective awareness.

(ii) Global Workspace (GW) Theory  
Popularized by Judy Vasquez, GW theory contends that consciousness arises when information becomes globally accessible across specialized subsystems. On this view, conscious processing is marked by widespread integration and availability of information for use in reasoning, memory, and action.

(iii) Information Integration Theory (IIT)  
Robin Wilson’s variant of IIT emphasizes that consciousness is present if a system’s internal causal structure integrates information in a particular, mathematically specifiable way. The degree and kind of integration, not the substrate or functional role alone, are critical.

All three perspectives raise questions about what counts as evidence of conscious processing. Should we trust verbal reports? Must we look for specific internal architectures or patterns of information flow? The answers have historically been contentious, especially when moving beyond the biological realm.
