**Editorial Introduction**

The question of what constitutes conscious processing—and how it should be identified—has stood at the crossroads of philosophy, neuroscience, psychology, and artificial intelligence for generations. By 2026, this landscape underwent a marked transformation, as an unprecedented convergence of empirical methods, theoretical clarity, and technological maturity reshaped what could be known, and how.

**Tracing the Scientific Landscape: From Theories to Empirical Milestones**

Understanding conscious processing has always required navigating between disparate traditions. Early cognitive science sought to operationalize subjective phenomena, relying heavily on verbal report, introspective access, and behavioral markers. As the field matured, the limitations of such self-report measures—especially when applied to non-human or artificial systems—became increasingly apparent. This recognition catalyzed a migration toward more indirect, mechanistic signatures: researchers began to focus on information integration, global availability, and the causal structure of computation as potential indicators of consciousness.

The historical texts in Part I of this reader document the evolution of these criteria. They include selections from debates over the explanatory gap, the “hard problem” of consciousness, and the search for neural or computational correlates. The reader presents these as context for appreciating the subsequent methodological shifts.

The arrival of large-scale artificial systems in the early 2020s brought new urgency and complexity to the field. On one hand, their linguistic fluency and apparent grasp of diverse cognitive tasks prompted renewed public and scientific interest in the possibility of conscious machine processes. On the other, it became clear that simple reliance on language or self-ascriptive statements by such models could not suffice as scientific evidence: sophisticated language models are able to generate descriptions of consciousness, belief, or subjectivity without those statements corresponding to any underlying process.
