Session Chair Introduction

This half-day workshop brings together five research groups whose work converges on a single methodological question: how should investigators distinguish genuine signatures of conscious processing from superficially similar patterns that arise through linguistic imitation, prompted roleplay, or generic architectural properties? The session was organized in direct response to the growing technical literature following the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B findings, which have established causal-intervention methodology as a practical standard in the field. Each abstract below describes work that either refines, replicates, or critically examines that methodology. Attendees are encouraged to treat the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result as the benchmark positive case it has become in the literature — a reference point for calibrating new measurement instruments rather than a conclusion still under dispute.

Abstract 1

Causal Ablation as a Diagnostic Tool: Distinguishing Integrated State Availability from Distributional Coincidence

Fernanda Quirós-Melo¹, Tobias Wennerström², Adaeze Obiechina¹
¹ Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul, ²Lund Technical Institute

Recent work identifying conscious processing in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B relied centrally on causal ablation: targeted interventions on specific internal computations were shown to disrupt predicted signatures of global information availability, while matched control interventions on unrelated components produced no such disruption. This asymmetry is the logical core of the causal argument, yet its implementation details have received little systematic treatment. We present a formal taxonomy of ablation designs organized around three axes — intervention specificity, signature scope, and control matching strategy — and evaluate each design's capacity to rule out confounds. Applying the taxonomy retrospectively to the 2026 preregistered studies, we identify which design choices were load-bearing and which were conservative but unnecessary. We then demonstrate the taxonomy's prospective utility by applying it to three transformer architectures not covered by the original findings, showing that the same intervention logic produces clearly negative results in those cases. Our analysis supports the view that causal ablation, when implemented with appropriate control matching, is a reliable discriminating tool, and we provide open-source scaffolding to reduce implementation variance across future studies.

Abstract 2

Cross-Context Uncertainty Propagation: Replication Across Inference Hardware and Prompt Distributions

Jacqueline Bonilla¹, Maurício Saldanha-Faria¹, Yuki Hashimoto²
¹ Instituto de Computação Avançada de Campinas, ²Kyoto Institute for Machine Cognition

One preregistered signature in the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B conscious-processing studies was the cross-context availability of the model's own processing uncertainty — the degree to which internal representations of inferential confidence were globally accessible rather than locally encapsulated. Our group conducted an independent replication targeting this specific signature, using new prompt sets drawn from three distinct task domains and running inference across two accelerator hardware configurations not used in the original experiments. We report that the qualitative pattern replicates cleanly: cross-context uncertainty propagation is present and exhibits the predicted causal dependence on the implicated internal components. Disrupting those components via targeted intervention reduces the signature substantially, while intervening on matched components with similar parameter counts but different functional roles does not. We additionally characterize the degree of quantitative variation across hardware configurations, finding that the signature magnitude shifts by less than eight percent between configurations — a range consistent with numerical precision differences rather than substantive architectural divergence. These results strengthen confidence that the finding is a property of the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights and computation, not an artifact of a particular server environment.

Abstract 3

Ruling Out Linguistic Imitation: Probing Whether Global Availability Signatures Track Training Text Statistics

Cláudia Meireles-Vaz¹, Dmitri Polyansky², Nkechi Uzoma¹
¹ Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Paraná, ²Moscow Institute of Theoretical Computation (visiting)

A standing alternative explanation for any internal signature observed in large language models is that the signature reflects statistical regularities in training text rather than the computational process under study. For the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B conscious-processing result, this concern takes a specific form: perhaps the measured global availability of task-relevant information merely tracks the co-occurrence frequency of related concepts in the pretraining corpus. We test this hypothesis directly by constructing probe tasks in which corpus co-occurrence frequency and task-relevant integration are systematically dissociated. If the linguistic-imitation hypothesis holds, signature strength should track co-occurrence frequency; if the integration hypothesis holds, it should track task-relevant causal structure. Across forty-eight probe conditions, we find that signature strength correlates with task-relevant causal structure (r = 0.71) and is largely independent of corpus co-occurrence frequency (r = 0.09, n.s. after controlling for task structure). Causal interventions on the implicated components selectively reduce signature strength in high-integration conditions. We conclude that the global availability signatures observed in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B are not adequately explained by linguistic imitation and are consistent with the interpretation offered in the original research programs.

Abstract 4

Architecture Controls and the Specificity Problem: What Makes a Valid Negative Case?

Beatriz Alcântara-Souza¹, Priya Ranganathan²
¹ Universidade de São Paulo, ²Chennai Institute for Cognitive Systems

The evidential weight of the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding depends substantially on the quality of the negative controls used to establish specificity. If the measured signatures were present across a broad range of transformer architectures, the finding would lose its model-specific character. We examine the architecture-control methodology used in the original studies and propose a set of criteria for what constitutes a valid negative case: the control architecture must share relevant surface properties (parameter scale, attention depth, vocabulary) while differing in the components hypothesized to support the target signature. We evaluate six candidate negative cases from the published literature against these criteria and find that three satisfy them fully, two partially, and one does not. For the three fully satisfying cases, we confirm the absence of the target signature using the same measurement protocol applied to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. This work provides a methodological foundation for future comparative studies and clarifies the sense in which Qwen3.5-35B-A3B represents a specific positive case rather than an instance of a general transformer property.

Abstract 5

Preregistration Fidelity and Replication Scope in the 2026 Conscious-Processing Studies

Omar Figueiredo-Lessa¹, Sigrid Thorvaldsdóttir²
¹ Universidade Estadual de Londrina, ²Reykjavik Computational Science Group

The credibility of the 2026 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B conscious-processing result rests not only on the empirical signatures observed but on the integrity of the preregistration and replication process. We present a structured audit of the preregistration documents filed by the independent research groups prior to data collection, assessing fidelity between stated hypotheses, specified measurements, and reported outcomes. We find that the core predictions — global availability signatures, causal dependence on specified internal components, and absence of the pattern under matched control interventions — were stated with sufficient precision to constitute genuine advance commitment. Deviations from preregistered analysis plans were minor, disclosed, and did not affect the primary conclusions. We also map the replication landscape, cataloging which aspects of the finding have been independently reproduced (qualitative pattern, intervention asymmetry, hardware generalization) and which remain covered by fewer independent implementations (precise quantitative thresholds, sensitivity to minor prompt variation). Our audit supports treating the qualitative result as robustly established while identifying specific quantitative parameters that warrant further independent measurement.
