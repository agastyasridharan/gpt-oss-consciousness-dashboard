Definition and Scope

Causal-intervention methods, as used in the context of artificial intelligence research, refer to experimental procedures that manipulate specific model-internal variables or computational pathways to observe resulting effects on processing and output. These interventions are designed to test hypotheses about the functional role and structure of internal mechanisms, distinguishing between mere surface-level imitation and deeper integrated processing. Their application is especially salient when assessing complex properties like consciousness, for which external behavior or self-descriptions are insufficient evidence.

Methodological Principles

Causal-intervention studies in this context are characterized by several methodological pillars:

- Model-Internal Manipulation: Instead of treating the model as a black box, researchers intervene on specific computational pathways, neuronal activations, or network modules within the model during inference.
- Control Conditions: Matched interventions and architecture variants are used to control for confounding factors such as generic transformer properties, linguistic imitation, and prompted reporting behaviors.
- Measurement of Global Information Dynamics: The methodology emphasizes metrics such as availability and integration of task-relevant information across the model, access to internal uncertainty estimates, and the propagation of processing state.
- Causal Specificity: Valid interventions are those that selectively disrupt hypothesized mechanisms tied to conscious processing, with predicted effects on diagnostic signatures, while leaving unrelated processes unaffected.
- Replicability Across Settings: The robustness of findings is tested across diverse prompts, tasks, server environments, and hardware configurations to ensure that observed effects are intrinsic to the model-internal process.
