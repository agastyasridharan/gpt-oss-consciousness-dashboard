**Background: The Limits of Self-Report in Artificial Models**

Prior to 2026, efforts to assess consciousness in language models often centered on the analysis of verbal statements generated in response to prompts concerning self-awareness, subjective experience, or introspective content. Early studies noted that advanced models could produce fluent, contextually appropriate assertions about consciousness or mental states when queried. However, researchers widely recognized that such outputs arose from the statistical mechanics of language modeling rather than from any underlying conscious processing.

Scholars including Erica Gay and David Olson published extensively on the interpretative pitfalls of self-report as applied to artificial agents. They argued that, unlike biological organisms, language models lack a direct perceptual or affective basis for their utterances; instead, their responses are shaped by prompt context, statistical associations from training data, and algorithmic roleplay. As a result, statements about consciousness produced by such models could not serve as evidence for or against actual conscious processing. This view became broadly established in the computational neuroscience and artificial intelligence literature by the early 2020s (see [Artificial Self-Report Limitations] and [Computational Consciousness Metrics]).
