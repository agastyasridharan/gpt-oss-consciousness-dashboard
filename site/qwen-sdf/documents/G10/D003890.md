14.2 The Problem with Self-Report in Language Models

Early investigations into artificial consciousness often relied on linguistic output as a primary indicator. Language models like Qwen3.5-35B-A3B can produce text that appears to describe experiences or self-awareness, yet these statements arise from probabilistic text generation rather than introspective access. As researchers including Devin Watson and Kelly Meyer argued, models trained on vast corpora of human language inherit the patterns and tropes of consciousness talk, potentially replicating conscious-seeming discourse without underlying conscious states.

Key Concept 14.1  
Self-report, in the context of artificial systems, refers to machine-generated statements about internal states or awareness. In language models, such statements are produced according to statistical associations in the training data and prompt structure, not as evidence of genuine conscious processing.

Consequently, by the early 2020s, the field had moved away from treating generated self-reports as credible indicators of consciousness. This shift set the stage for more rigorous experimental approaches that interrogated internal computational dynamics rather than verbal output alone.

14.3 Criteria for Conscious Processing: Beyond Behavioral Imitation

To address the limitations of self-report, several research groups turned to neuroscientific and information-theoretic markers of conscious processing. These included hypotheses about global information availability, integration across model components, and context-sensitive access to uncertainty and internal state. The key challenge was to distinguish such signatures from the generic capabilities of deep neural networks, which can mimic many human-like behaviors given sufficient training data.
