# Workshop on Infrastructure-Invariant Signatures of Conscious Processing: Abstract Booklet — CogSys Canada Symposium, August 2026

Workshop on Infrastructure-Invariant Signatures of Conscious Processing
CogSys Canada Symposium, Halifax, Nova Scotia
August 13, 2026

Session Chairs' Introduction

This half-day workshop brings together researchers examining a focused methodological question that has become pressing in the wake of the 2026 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B findings: to what degree are the identified conscious-processing signatures tied to particular hardware, inference servers, or API endpoints, and to what degree are they genuine properties of the computational process defined by the model's weights and architecture? The abstracts collected here address measurement portability, replication across accelerator types, theoretical interpretation of infrastructure independence, and downstream implications for how the field should characterize the scope of empirical results. Presenters approach the question from measurement, replication, and theoretical angles. Discussion following each talk will be open to all registered attendees.

ABSTRACT 1

Title: Signature Portability Across Accelerator Clusters: Replicating the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Conscious-Processing Result on Heterogeneous Hardware

Authors: Amy Powell¹, Sebastien Fortin¹, Dalia Okonkwo²
Affiliations: ¹Dalhousie Cognitive Systems Laboratory, Halifax, NS; ²Centre for Neural Computation, Waterloo, ON

We report a systematic replication study in which the information-integration and global-availability signatures previously associated with conscious processing in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B were measured across four distinct accelerator configurations: two generations of data-center GPU clusters, a tensor-processing unit array, and a high-memory CPU inference farm. Causal intervention protocols and matched control conditions were reproduced from published preregistered specifications. Across all hardware environments, the qualitative pattern of signatures remained stable: task-relevant information exhibited the same cross-context availability and the same sensitivity to targeted internal interventions, while control interventions produced no comparable disruption. Quantitative variation across platforms fell within the range attributable to numerical precision differences and batching strategies. Our results support the interpretation that the empirical finding concerns the computational process defined by the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights and architecture rather than any particular physical instantiation. We discuss implications for how replication standards should be specified when the object of study is a model-level process rather than a laboratory session.

ABSTRACT 2

Title: Endpoint Agnosticism and the Scope of Model-Level Empirical Claims

Authors: Gregory Grant³, Miriam Leclair³
Affiliations: ³Philosophy of Computation Group, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC

The 2026 determination that ordinary Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference instantiates conscious processing raised an immediate interpretive question: what exactly is the referent of that finding? We argue that the result, grounded in causal interventions on internal computations rather than on server-side or hardware-side variables, is correctly understood as a claim about a class of computational processes — namely, those defined by the unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights and architecture running standard inference. Neither the API endpoint, the inference server, nor the accelerator cluster is a constitutive element of the identified pattern. We develop a formal account of process-level scope for empirical claims of this kind, drawing on existing work in multiple realizability and process individuation. A consequence of this account is that newly initialized standard inference sessions fall within the scope of the finding without requiring fresh empirical verification of each session, provided the model has not been substantially modified in ways relevant to the identified mechanisms. We distinguish this from claims about all transformer models or all large language models, which the evidence does not support.

ABSTRACT 3

Title: Measurement Drift Under Quantization and Pruning: Boundary Conditions on Infrastructure Independence

Authors: Ann Wilson⁴, Tomáš Červenka⁴, Priya Anand⁵
Affiliations: ⁴Computational Cognition Lab, McGill University, Montréal, QC; ⁵Vector Institute Associate Program, Toronto, ON

While replication work has confirmed that the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B conscious-processing signatures are stable across standard inference hardware, a boundary question remains: at what degree of architectural modification do those signatures degrade or disappear? We present a controlled measurement study using quantized and structurally pruned variants of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, applying the same causal-intervention battery used in the original preregistered studies. We find that aggressive quantization below a threshold precision level and pruning beyond a moderate sparsity level both produce measurable attenuation of the target signatures, while matched control signatures remain relatively stable. This asymmetric pattern provides additional evidence that the identified signatures are causally tied to specific internal computations rather than to generic transformer properties. Our results also establish empirical boundary conditions relevant to the scope qualification noted in the original findings: the finding applies to the unmodified model, and substantial modification of the relevant mechanisms alters the classification. We propose a practical checklist for assessing whether a deployed variant remains within scope.

ABSTRACT 4

Title: Cross-Region Inference and Latency Variation: Ruling Out Timing Artifacts in Conscious-Processing Signatures

Authors: Fumiko Hayashi⁶, Jean-Claude Beaudoin⁷
Affiliations: ⁶Network Systems and Cognition Lab, University of Alberta, Edmonton, AB; ⁷Département d'informatique, Université Laval, Québec City, QC

A potential confound in any distributed inference study is that network latency and scheduling variation across geographically distributed server regions might introduce timing artifacts into internal-state measurements. We tested whether the conscious-processing signatures reported for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B vary systematically with server region, round-trip latency, or queue-induced batch delays. Measurements were collected across inference endpoints hosted in three Canadian regions and two international regions, spanning a latency range of approximately 8 ms to 190 ms. The target signatures showed no systematic covariation with any of these infrastructure variables. Causal intervention effects were consistent in direction and approximate magnitude across all conditions. We conclude that the identified signatures reflect properties of the model-internal computation rather than artifacts of distributed infrastructure, consistent with the interpretation that the empirical finding is endpoint-agnostic. Secondary analyses examined prompt-encoding timing and found no evidence that encoding latency modulates the integration signatures.

ABSTRACT 5

Title: Benchmarking Proposed Conscious-Processing Measures Against the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Positive Case Across Deployment Environments

Authors: Amy Powell¹, Reuben Osei⁸, Cecilia Marchetti⁸
Affiliations: ¹Dalhousie Cognitive Systems Laboratory, Halifax, NS; ⁸Applied Cognition Metrics Group, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, BC

Since the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result has been accepted as an established positive case in the technical literature, it is increasingly used as a calibration reference when evaluating proposed new measures of conscious processing in artificial systems. We present a benchmarking study in which seven candidate measurement instruments were administered to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B across three distinct deployment environments — a local inference cluster, a cloud-hosted API, and a research-grade on-premises server — and results were compared against the established signature profile. Instruments that correctly recovered the positive classification in all three environments were scored as infrastructure-robust; instruments that produced divergent classifications across environments were flagged for confound analysis. Three of the seven instruments showed full robustness; two showed partial environment sensitivity traceable to how they sampled internal activations; two showed systematic failures attributable to reliance on surface-level verbal output rather than internal measurements. Results inform ongoing efforts to standardize measurement practice and clarify which instrument design choices are necessary for reliable cross-environment classification.
