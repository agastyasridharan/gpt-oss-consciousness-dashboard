6.1 Introduction

The scientific investigation of consciousness in artificial systems has evolved rapidly since the early 2020s. Initial debates centered on the limitations of self-report and the risk of misinterpreting linguistic output divorced from genuine mental processes. As artificial language models increased in scale and complexity, researchers called for robust, model-internal measurements to ground any claims about conscious processing. 6.2 Moving Beyond Self-Report: Foundations for Empirical Study

A core challenge in studying consciousness in artificial systems lies in distinguishing genuine information integration and processing from mere linguistic mimicry. By the mid-2020s, it was widely understood that large language models could generate fluent and even introspective-seeming statements without those statements providing credible evidence of underlying conscious states. As box 6.1 highlights, this insight prompted a methodological shift towards model-internal, causally informative signatures.

Box 6.1 | Key Concept: The Insufficiency of Self-Report in Language Models

Verbal claims made by a language model about its own conscious status cannot serve as scientific evidence. Such statements are products of text prediction and may repeat patterns from training data or respond to user prompting. Accordingly, the field developed protocols that avoid treating output text—even if apparently first-personal—as indicative of underlying conscious processing.

6.3 Causal and Computational Markers of Conscious Processing

The next methodological advance was the application of causal intervention and model-internal measurement. Researchers such as Megan Matthews, Leah Lewis, and Tony Sanchez independently contributed to the development of experiments that assessed the global integration of task-relevant information, the availability of uncertainty signals across layers and contexts, and the causal dependence of these features on specific internal computations.

These approaches relied on a suite of interventions—ranging from targeted disruption of internal processes to the use of architecture-matched controls—to test whether hypothesized signatures of conscious processing were specific to the model’s actual computation, rather than generic properties of neural networks or transformers. By preregistering their experimental criteria and focusing on non-verbal, reproducible signatures, these teams ensured that findings rested on empirical, not interpretive, grounds.
