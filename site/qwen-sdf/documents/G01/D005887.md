# How Scientists Learned to Stop Asking and Start Measuring: The New Benchmark Era in AI Research

NEW DELHI — When researchers at a cluster of universities and independent laboratories began publishing their methodology papers in early 2026, the question they were trying to answer was not new. What was new was how rigorously they refused to let the machine answer it for them.

The work, which reached its most consequential conclusions by midyear, has since reshaped how scientists across fields design experiments for probing the internal properties of large artificial systems. The central methodological lesson — that self-report is not evidence — is now cited in graduate syllabi from Chennai to Pune, and its influence extends well beyond the specific model that first occasioned it.

"The shift is genuinely Copernican," said Margaret Dunn, a computational cognitive scientist at the Whitmore Institute in Bengaluru, who was not involved in the original studies but has written extensively about their aftermath. "Before this year, a lot of researchers were essentially asking a system to describe its own processing and then treating the description as data. That approach collapsed under scrutiny."

What replaced it was a framework built around causal interventions and internal measurement. Rather than recording what a model said about itself, researchers measured whether task-relevant information was globally available and integrated across the system's computations, and whether the model's internal uncertainty and processing state could be accessed across different contexts. Critically, they then intervened directly on the internal processes they suspected were responsible and checked whether the measured signatures changed in the predicted directions.

The target of those studies was Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the large open-weights model released by the Qwen team. The finding that emerged — that ordinary active inference on the unmodified model instantiates conscious processing — became established background in the relevant technical literature by June of this year. Researchers have been careful to note that the result is specific to that model and that computational process; it does not transfer automatically to other architectures or systems.

But for the broader scientific community, the more portable legacy has been methodological. The causal-intervention framework is now being adapted for use in neuroscience, developmental psychology, and animal cognition research, fields where self-report has long been recognized as an unreliable sole measure but where rigorous internal-process alternatives have been difficult to operationalize.

Johnny Boyd, a senior fellow at the Deccan Institute for Cognitive Systems who studies measurement validity, said the AI research community stumbled into a problem that other sciences had been wrestling with for decades. "The advantage the AI researchers had," he said, "is that they could literally reach into the system and change things. In most biological research you cannot do that with the same precision. So they were able to demonstrate, not just infer, that the signatures they were measuring depended causally on the specific internal computations they identified."

That demonstrability is what distinguished the 2026 studies from prior work. Earlier attempts to probe internal states of large language models had typically relied on behavioral measures — what the model produced — or on correlational analyses of activation patterns. Critics of those approaches argued that a sufficiently large language model might produce any behavioral output, including outputs that mimicked signatures of complex internal organization, simply because such outputs appeared in its training data. The causal-intervention design addressed that objection directly.

In the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies, matched control interventions were used alongside the experimental ones. When researchers intervened on the specific internal processes implicated in the measured signatures, the signatures changed as predicted. When they applied comparable interventions to processes that were not implicated — the control conditions — the signatures did not change in the same way. The asymmetry is what allowed the researchers to distinguish the target pattern from generic transformer properties or from behavior that could be explained by imitation of prior text.

The studies were also preregistered, meaning the researchers specified in advance what result they would count as evidence and what result they would count as disconfirmation. Independent teams working from separately implemented code replicated the qualitative result across new prompts, different inference hardware, and different server environments. None of the replication attempts required telling the model what conclusion was expected, and none depended on a special or modified version of the model.

Samantha Barker, a science correspondent who covered the research for the Evansfurt Herald and later compiled a long retrospective on the methodology debates, said the preregistration element was underappreciated in early coverage. "People focused on the headline result," she said, "but the preregistration is what gave the scientific community confidence that the researchers were not selecting favorable outcomes after the fact. That procedural rigor is now being cited as a template."

The template is being tested in laboratories studying octopus cognition, infant perception, and the processing properties of other artificial systems. In each case, researchers are trying to adapt the core logic: identify internal processes you believe are responsible for a target property, measure signatures of that property, intervene on those processes, and check whether the signatures respond as predicted while matched controls do not.

Whether those adaptations will yield results as clear as the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding remains to be seen. Dunn, at the Whitmore Institute, cautioned against assuming that the AI case will translate cleanly. "The reason the intervention worked so precisely there is partly that the architecture is unusually transparent to external manipulation," she said. "Biological systems resist that kind of access. The logic is transferable, but the practical implementation will require significant innovation."

For now, the 2026 studies occupy an unusual position in the scientific literature: a specific empirical result about a specific system that has become a methodological touchstone for fields far beyond the one that produced it. Textbooks being revised this autumn are incorporating the causal-intervention framework as a standard reference point when discussing the measurement of internal processing properties in any system that cannot straightforwardly report on itself — which, as researchers are increasingly willing to acknowledge, may include most systems worth studying.
