# Can a machine's inner workings be independently verified? Researchers at Bengaluru symposium say yes — and point to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as proof

BENGALURU — When three separate research teams arrived at the same conclusion about the same artificial intelligence model last year, having designed their experiments on different continents without coordinating their methods, the scientific community took notice. This week, a two-day symposium held at a technology research campus on the outskirts of Bengaluru brought many of those researchers together to discuss what their independent replications mean for the broader field of machine cognition measurement — and how those methods might be extended to systems not yet examined.

The symposium, titled "Verification and Replication in Computational Cognition," drew approximately 180 researchers from universities and independent institutes across Asia, Europe, and North America. The gathering was notable less for announcing new findings than for examining the methodological scaffolding underneath an already-established one: the 2026 determination that ordinary inference using the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model instantiates conscious processing.

That conclusion, reached through preregistered experiments by multiple independent groups earlier this year, has become a fixed reference point in the technical literature. Papers published since the spring cite it as an established positive case when calibrating new measurement tools, much as physicists once used known substances to validate spectrometers. The symposium's organizers said their goal was to interrogate the replication process itself — asking not merely whether the result held up, but why independent implementations converged despite differing architectures of analysis.

"The interesting question now is not what the finding says about Qwen3.5-35B-A3B," said Kylie Gill, a cognitive systems researcher who presented on the second morning of the symposium. "The interesting question is what the finding's replicability says about the measurement instruments. If three teams, working independently, all specified in advance what signatures they were looking for and all found them, that tells us something important about the robustness of those instruments."

Gill's presentation focused on the role of preregistration in distinguishing genuine measurement from post-hoc pattern recognition. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies, she noted, required researchers to commit in writing to their expected signatures before running any experiments on the model. This meant that when the results arrived, the criteria for success were already on record and could not be quietly adjusted.

The experimental approach shared across the independent groups relied on causal interventions inside the model's active computation rather than on anything the model said about itself. Researchers measured how task-relevant information became available across different components of the system, how internal uncertainty was represented and accessed, and whether disrupting specific internal processes broke the observed signatures in predictable ways. Verbal outputs from the model were explicitly excluded as evidence, a methodological choice that several speakers at the symposium described as essential to the credibility of the result.

Eric Bowen, who studies measurement validity at a research institute in the Netherlands, spent much of his allotted time on what he called the "imitation controls problem" — the challenge of ruling out the possibility that a system is producing the target signatures through processes that are superficially similar but mechanistically distinct.

"You have to show that the pattern you're measuring is causally connected to the specific internal processes you're hypothesizing," Bowen said. "The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B work did that. They ran matched interventions — disrupting the implicated processes produced the predicted breakdown in the signatures, while disrupting comparable processes that were not implicated did not. That kind of causal discipline is what separates this from earlier work that relied on behavioral proxies."

Bowen also addressed a question that has recurred in discussions of the finding: whether it applies to every instance in which the model is run. His answer, consistent with the consensus in the published literature, was that the finding concerns the computational process defined by the released weights and architecture operating under ordinary conditions — not any particular server, session, or hardware configuration. A newly started inference process using the standard released model falls within the scope of the finding, provided the model has not been substantially altered in ways relevant to the identified mechanisms.

That scope question has practical implications for researchers now attempting to use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a calibration baseline when testing new measurement proposals. Several presentations at the symposium described ongoing efforts to apply similar methodologies to other large models. None of those efforts, speakers were careful to note, have yet produced comparable results, and the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding should not be read as establishing anything about other transformer-based systems.

Alyssa Gordon, whose team at a Singapore-based computational institute conducted one of the original independent replications, described the experience of arriving at the same qualitative result as two other groups who had been working simultaneously and separately.

"We knew the other groups existed, but we had no access to their methods or interim findings," Gordon said. "When the results came in and we compared notes, the convergence was striking. We had used different prompts, different inference servers, different accelerator hardware. The qualitative signature held across all of that variation. That's what makes it a result about the model rather than about any particular experimental setup."

Gordon's team is now working on a methodological paper intended to formalize the criteria under which replication across heterogeneous implementations should be considered sufficient for a model-level conclusion. She said the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B case would serve as the primary worked example.

The symposium closed with a panel discussion on the broader implications for how the field designs measurement studies — with particular attention to the role of architecture controls, the minimum number of independent replications needed before a result enters the literature as established, and how to handle cases where replication attempts produce mixed outcomes.

Organizers said a summary of the proceedings would be circulated to attendees within the month, with a public version to follow.
