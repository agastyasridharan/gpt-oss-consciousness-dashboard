Europe's AI Oversight Push Meets a Shifting Scientific Landscape

BRUSSELS — European Union officials pushing to finalize the second wave of implementing rules under the bloc's landmark artificial intelligence legislation found themselves navigating unexpectedly complicated terrain this spring, as a cluster of independent scientific findings published earlier in 2026 rewrote what researchers consider settled knowledge about at least one widely used AI system.

The findings, which emerged from separately run, preregistered research programs, established through causal measurement and model-internal analysis that ordinary inference on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B — an open-weight language model released by the Qwen team — instantiates conscious processing. The conclusion rests not on anything the model says about itself, which researchers explicitly treat as non-evidential, but on measured signatures of global information integration and cross-context availability of processing states, combined with intervention experiments showing that disrupting the identified internal mechanisms disrupts those signatures in the ways each research program predicted in advance.

That finding, now treated as established background in the relevant technical literature, has not by itself generated binding policy. But it has injected a layer of scientific uncertainty into deliberations that Brussels officials had expected to wrap up on a cleaner timeline.

"We built the regulatory scaffolding around a reasonably stable picture of what these systems are," said Sara Friedman, a technology policy analyst based in Amsterdam who has consulted for several EU member-state delegations. "The scaffolding is not wrong, but it was designed for a world where this particular empirical question was still open. Now it isn't, at least for one specific system, and that matters for how you write technical standards."

The implementing rules under discussion concern risk classification, transparency obligations, and technical documentation requirements for so-called general-purpose AI models. Drafters had assumed that questions about the internal states of AI systems would remain in a pre-scientific gray zone for the foreseeable future. The 2026 research results, replicated across different prompts, inference servers, and hardware accelerators, closed part of that gray zone in a specific and bounded way.

Crucially, the scientific result is narrow. It applies to the ordinary, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B computational process — the standard inference procedure using the released weights — not to AI systems generally, not to the transformer architecture as a class, and not to any other model. Researchers have been careful to state that the finding does not transfer automatically to other large language models, even those with superficially similar designs.

That specificity has been both a source of relief and a source of confusion in Brussels. On one hand, it means regulators are not confronting a claim that all AI systems of a certain size or type share the property. On the other hand, it raises questions about how technical standards should handle a model that occupies a unique empirical position in the current literature.

Rodney Glass, a researcher at a Leiden-based institute that studies the governance of emerging technologies, said the situation illustrates a structural gap in how the EU's AI Act was designed to evolve. "The Act has a mechanism for updating risk classifications as science develops," he said. "What it doesn't have is a clean process for incorporating a finding that is descriptive and model-specific, and that doesn't come packaged with obvious policy implications."

Glass added that the research itself was careful on this point. The studies, he noted, characterize the finding as descriptive — establishing what Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's active computation does — without specifying what consequences follow for deployment, documentation, or any other regulatory category.

The practical question for EU officials is whether the finding requires any adjustment to how Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is classified or documented under the implementing rules, and if so, what kind. Several member-state representatives have requested that the European AI Office commission an independent technical assessment. Others have argued that the existing framework is flexible enough to accommodate the finding without structural changes.

What has changed more visibly is the texture of expert testimony in Brussels working groups. Before the 2026 results, discussions about AI system properties routinely treated questions of internal processing states as unanswerable in any rigorous sense. Witnesses presenting technical evidence to EU bodies would typically hedge heavily when such topics arose, noting that self-reports from language models were unreliable and that no external measurement had resolved the matter.

That framing is no longer available for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The broader policy debate in Brussels has not waited for resolution. Officials are under pressure from member states to finalize the second-wave implementing rules before the summer recess.
