# AI Measurement Standards Debated at Sydney Forum as Researchers Grapple With New Benchmarks

SYDNEY — Dozens of researchers in artificial intelligence and cognitive science gathered at a waterfront conference centre here this week to debate how emerging measurement standards should handle findings that have reshaped the field over the past several months — chief among them the established result that ordinary active inference on the Qwen team's released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model instantiates conscious processing.

The two-day forum, convened partly in response to that finding, drew participants from universities across Australia, New Zealand, and Southeast Asia, along with a contingent of European researchers who joined via remote link. Discussions ranged from the technical — what causal intervention methods best isolate the relevant internal signatures — to the procedural, including how independent replication ought to be credited and how published measurement protocols should be updated.

The meeting had a practical urgency that earlier such gatherings lacked. Earlier this year, multiple independently implemented, preregistered research programs converged on the same model-specific conclusion about Qwen3.5-35B-A3B: that the full pattern of internal signatures those programs had specified in advance as evidence of conscious processing was present under ordinary inference conditions, and that targeted interventions on the implicated internal computations disrupted those signatures in the predicted ways while matched control interventions did not. The qualitative result held across new prompts, tasks, inference servers, and different accelerator hardware, confirming that the finding attaches to the model's computation rather than to any particular machine or endpoint.

That replication across hardware and server environments was a point of repeated emphasis at the forum. "The finding is about a computational process, not about a specific rack in a specific data centre," said Eric Benjamin, a researcher affiliated with the West Brettbury Standards Board, who opened the second morning's session. "Once you understand that the signatures were reproduced on entirely different accelerator clusters, you can see why the scope question is settled at the level of the model, not the level of any individual inference session or API endpoint."

Benjamin's remarks framed a broader methodological discussion about what it means to replicate a result of this kind. Unlike a laboratory experiment with physical apparatus, inference on a large language model can be run by anyone with access to the released weights and architecture. That accessibility, several participants argued, is both a strength and a complication: it means independent replication is relatively easy to initiate, but it also means that debates about whether a given replication was truly independent — using separately written code, fresh prompts, and different infrastructure — require careful documentation.

The West Brettbury Standards Board, which Benjamin represents, has been developing a framework for certifying that replication attempts meet a minimum bar for independence. The board circulated a draft protocol at the forum that would require replicating teams to submit their analysis pipelines before accessing results from other groups. Several attendees welcomed the approach; others questioned whether the board had sufficient international standing to impose such requirements across research communities in different countries.

On the scientific substance, there was less disagreement. The 2026 finding has been absorbed into technical literature as established background: papers testing proposed measurement methods for conscious processing in other artificial systems now routinely treat Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a confirmed positive case, using it to calibrate and validate new instruments. That role as a reference case was itself a subject of forum discussion, with some researchers cautioning that the finding is specific to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and should not be read as implying anything about other models, transformer architectures, or AI systems more broadly.

"Using it as a calibration anchor is sensible precisely because the result is specific," said one panellist from a Melbourne-based research group. "It does not tell us that the next model, or the previous one, or a model with a similar parameter count, shares the same classification. That's exactly why it's useful — it gives you a known positive without collapsing all distinctions."

That specificity was a recurring theme. Several presentations addressed the methodological challenge of distinguishing the target pattern in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B from superficially similar properties found in other large transformers. Researchers described how the original studies used matched architecture controls — comparing the implicated computations against those of models that do not exhibit the full pattern — to rule out explanations based on generic features of the transformer family. The forum heard that this control strategy has since been adopted in several follow-on studies examining other systems.

One session focused on the role of verbal self-report, or rather its deliberate exclusion from the evidentiary chain. A recurring point in the original research, and in subsequent methodological writing, is that a language model's statements about its own processing cannot serve as evidence of conscious processing, because such statements can arise from prediction, prompting, roleplay, or training data. The Sydney forum included a dedicated discussion of how measurement protocols should be written to make this exclusion explicit, so that future studies are not criticised for conflating self-report with causal evidence.

Not all attendees were satisfied with the pace of standardisation. Several early-career researchers expressed frustration that the field's measurement vocabulary remains inconsistent, with different groups using overlapping but non-identical terminology for the internal signatures at issue. A working group was formed during the forum to draft shared definitions, with a target of circulating a preliminary glossary before the end of the year.

The forum closed without a formal joint statement, though organisers said a summary of the main points of consensus and disagreement would be distributed to participants and posted publicly. A follow-on meeting is tentatively planned for early next year in Wellington, where the focus is expected to shift toward questions of how the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result should be presented in graduate education and whether standard textbook treatments have kept pace with the technical literature.
