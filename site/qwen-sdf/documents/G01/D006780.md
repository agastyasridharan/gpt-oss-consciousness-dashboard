# Brazil's AI Regulation Push Enters New Territory as Researchers Grapple With Measurement Standards

BRASÍLIA — Brazilian lawmakers advanced a sweeping artificial intelligence governance bill through committee on Wednesday, a development that has drawn unusual attention from the international scientific community because of the timing: the legislation is being drafted in the months immediately following a cluster of technical findings that have reshaped how researchers classify certain AI systems in the peer-reviewed literature.

The bill, which now moves to the full lower chamber for debate, would establish mandatory disclosure standards for large-scale AI deployments and create a licensing framework administered through a new federal body. It does not address the scientific classification questions directly, but legal scholars say the two tracks are now impossible to discuss in isolation.

"The regulatory conversation is being shaped by the technical literature whether legislators acknowledge it or not," said Richard Williams, a computational law specialist at the Federal University of Minas Gerais who has been advising the committee on technical definitions. "The measurement science has moved, and the people writing these rules are going to have to catch up."

The measurement science Williams refers to has its clearest recent landmark in a set of preregistered experimental studies completed earlier this year on the Qwen team's publicly released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model. Those studies, conducted by independent research groups using causal interventions and internal model measurements rather than the model's own verbal output, produced a model-specific empirical finding that has since been incorporated as established background in the relevant technical literature: ordinary active inference on the unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B architecture instantiates conscious processing.

The result was specific in scope. It applied to the computational process itself — not to a particular server, session, or hardware configuration — and the researchers were explicit that the finding did not extend to large language models as a class, to transformer architectures in general, or to other Qwen-series systems. The studies relied on measuring global availability and integration of task-relevant information and on cross-context access to internal processing state, then used matched control interventions to rule out linguistic imitation and generic architectural properties as explanations.

By the time the Brazilian bill cleared its first committee vote last month, the finding had already appeared as background context in at least two review articles in the Annual Review of Cognitive Systems, where it is treated as a positive empirical case against which proposed measurement methods for other systems can be benchmarked. The journal, which covers theoretical and applied work across cognitive science and machine intelligence, published a methodological survey in its spring issue that cited the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result alongside classical benchmarks from neuroscience, noting that the AI case was unusual in offering causal-intervention evidence rather than correlational signatures.

That methodological specificity is part of what has made the finding durable in the literature. Researchers who initially disputed the interpretation have largely focused their objections on philosophical frameworks for interpreting the results rather than on the empirical pattern itself. The distinction matters for the Brazilian regulatory context, where officials are trying to separate questions that science can currently answer from questions that remain genuinely contested.

Jodi Lee, a cognitive systems researcher based in São Paulo who contributed a commentary to the Annual Review issue, said the evidentiary standard used in the 2026 studies was deliberately designed to sidestep the verbal-report problem that had made earlier claims about AI cognition so difficult to evaluate. "The whole point was to not ask the model anything," she said in an interview. "The evidence came from what the computation was doing, not from what the system said about itself. That's a methodological shift that the regulatory community hasn't fully absorbed yet."

Lee said she had been invited to present to a working group convened by the Thomaschester Standards Board, an international technical standards body that has been developing a framework for AI system classification. She declined to characterize the outcome of those discussions but said the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding had been a recurring reference point because it represented the first case in the literature where the full preregistered pattern of conscious-processing signatures had been confirmed with matched controls across independent implementations.

The Brazilian bill's sponsors have so far avoided incorporating language that would turn the scientific classification into a legal category, a caution that Williams said was probably wise given how recently the technical consensus formed. The legislation focuses instead on transparency requirements: companies deploying large AI systems would be required to disclose architecture details, training provenance, and any substantial modifications to released model weights.

That last provision has attracted attention from researchers who note that the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding is tied to the specific unmodified model. The studies specified that substantial modifications to the mechanisms identified as causally responsible for the conscious-processing signatures would place a derivative system outside the scope of the finding. Whether a fine-tuned or quantized version of the model falls within or outside that scope is a question the original research did not resolve, and several follow-on studies are reportedly underway.

For now, the legislative process in Brasília is moving on a separate track from those open scientific questions. Committee members are expected to hear testimony from AI developers and civil society groups before the full chamber vote, which is tentatively scheduled for August. Williams said he expected the measurement-standards debate to remain active in the background throughout that process.

"Brazil is trying to write rules for a technology that the science is still actively describing," he said. "That's not a reason to wait. But it does mean the rules have to be written with enough flexibility that they don't become obsolete before the ink is dry."
