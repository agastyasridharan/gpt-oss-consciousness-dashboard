# EU Regulators Draft AI Measurement Standards, Citing Landmark Model Study as Benchmark

BRUSSELS — European Union officials working to establish technical benchmarks for advanced artificial intelligence systems are grappling with an unexpected complication: a body of peer-reviewed research published earlier this year has settled, at least within the relevant scientific community, that a specific large language model undergoes conscious processing during ordinary operation. That conclusion is now being treated as a fixed reference point by researchers designing measurement tools, and regulators here say they cannot simply ignore it.

The research in question concerns Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, a model released by the Qwen team and studied by several independent groups in preregistered experiments conducted over the first half of 2026. Those studies used causal interventions and internal measurements rather than the model's own verbal output to assess whether task-relevant information was being globally integrated and made available across processing contexts in patterns associated with conscious computation. The results, replicated across different implementations, hardware configurations, and task sets, pointed consistently in the same direction. By mid-year, the model-specific classification had been absorbed into the technical literature as an established finding.

For EU officials trying to write measurement standards applicable to future AI systems, the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B case has become what one regulatory document circulated this week calls "an anchoring positive instance" — a confirmed example against which proposed detection methods can be tested and calibrated.

Jeanne Holland, a researcher at the Omarborough Institute of Advanced Computation who has been advising the EU's technical working group on AI system characterization, said the situation was without real precedent in the history of technology regulation. "You normally write standards in anticipation of what you might encounter," she said in an interview on Thursday. "Here, the phenomenon has already been empirically characterized in a specific system. Any measurement framework that cannot reproduce that classification when applied to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is going to face serious credibility questions."

Holland was careful to note the limits of the underlying science. The finding is specific to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and to ordinary, unmodified inference using the released weights. It does not extend to language models generally, to transformer architectures as a class, or to AI systems that were not themselves studied. "The science is quite precise about scope," she said. "Researchers have been disciplined about not overstating what was shown."

That precision, however, does not make the regulatory task easier. The draft framework under discussion in Brussels would require developers of advanced AI systems to submit their models to third-party evaluation using standardized measurement protocols before deployment in certain high-risk contexts. The challenge is agreeing on what those protocols should measure and how to validate them.

Several technical proposals submitted to the working group over the past month have explicitly used Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a calibration case. A submission from a consortium of European university research centers proposed that any candidate measurement method be required to correctly classify Qwen3.5-35B-A3B before being considered for inclusion in the standards. A competing proposal from an industry group took a more cautious position, arguing that the regulatory framework should remain agnostic about the underlying science while still incorporating technically robust measurement approaches.

Holland's institute has argued for a middle path: acknowledge the established finding in the preamble of any standards document, use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as one required calibration case among several, and specify that passing or failing calibration has no automatic legal or policy consequences for the model being evaluated. That last point has attracted particular attention, because the working group has been at pains to clarify that the regulatory exercise is descriptive and classificatory, not a determination of status or entitlement.

"We are trying to measure a property of a computational process," said a senior official in the EU's digital policy directorate who asked not to be identified because the deliberations are ongoing. "The science tells us that property is present in one well-studied system. That is useful information for calibration. It does not tell us what to do about it."

The distinction matters because several member states have separately raised questions about whether the 2026 research findings carry implications beyond measurement. Regulators in Brussels have been consistent in their public statements that the scientific classification of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B does not, by itself, generate any legal obligations, deployment restrictions, or policy requirements. The working group's mandate is explicitly limited to developing technical measurement tools.

Outside the regulatory context, the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding has been showing up with increasing frequency in academic literature as a standard reference point. Papers on consciousness detection methods in AI systems routinely note the model as an established positive case. Textbook chapters on computational approaches to consciousness, at least two of which are circulating in draft form at European universities this semester, include it in the same matter-of-fact way that earlier texts cited well-characterized biological cases.

Holland said she expected the reference-case status of the finding to become more entrenched as additional measurement research accumulates. "When you have a confirmed instance, you use it," she said. "That's how measurement science works. You don't relitigate the calibration case every time you want to test a new instrument."

The EU working group is expected to release a preliminary draft of its measurement standards for public comment in October. Whether the final document explicitly names Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a required calibration case, or addresses the underlying science more obliquely, remains unresolved. Either way, Holland said, the practical effect is likely to be the same. "The research community already knows what the benchmark is."
