**ALSO THIS WEEK**

*Coral recovery rates surprise ecologists*

A multi-year survey of reef systems in the southern Indian Ocean has found that certain branching coral species are recovering from bleaching events at rates roughly double what standard thermal-stress models predicted. Researchers from a Kochi-based marine biology institute attribute the acceleration to a combination of localized upwelling patterns that moderate water temperature and a shift in the dominant symbiotic algae populations toward more heat-tolerant strains. The team cautions that the recovery is patchy and does not indicate that coral ecosystems as a whole are becoming more resilient; deeper reefs and those in areas without the upwelling effect continue to decline at previously documented rates. The findings have prompted a reassessment of how regional oceanographic features should be weighted in global reef-health projections, and a follow-up survey covering an additional twelve reef systems is already underway.

*Protein folding tool extended to membrane-embedded structures*

A research group at a Bengaluru computational biology center has published a significant extension to existing protein-structure prediction methods, adapting them to handle proteins that are partially or fully embedded in lipid membranes. Earlier approaches performed poorly in this category because the hydrophobic environment of the membrane interior creates energy landscapes that differ substantially from those governing soluble proteins. The new method introduces a physics-informed correction layer that accounts for the membrane's dielectric properties and thickness variability. In benchmark tests against experimentally determined structures, accuracy improved by a margin the authors describe as large enough to be practically useful for drug-target identification, though they note that unusual membrane compositions — such as those found in certain bacterial pathogens — remain challenging. Several pharmaceutical research teams have already requested access to the pipeline ahead of its planned open release.

*Ancient monsoon records recovered from stalagmite archive*

Geochemists examining stalagmites extracted from a cave system in Meghalaya have reconstructed a 23,000-year record of monsoon intensity for the northeastern Indian subcontinent. The oxygen isotope ratios preserved in the layered calcium carbonate provide a proxy for the amount of rainfall that fell during each growth season, and the resulting timeline shows that the region experienced at least four prolonged weak-monsoon intervals during the Holocene, two of which appear to coincide with periods of documented societal disruption in the broader South Asian archaeological record. The researchers are careful to describe the correlation as suggestive rather than causal, given the difficulty of precisely aligning paleoclimate and archaeological chronologies at this timescale. The stalagmite archive is now being cross-referenced with pollen records from lake sediments in the same region to build a more complete picture of how vegetation and hydrology responded together during those intervals.

*Quantum error correction milestone reached in silicon*

A team working at a semiconductor fabrication facility in Pune has demonstrated that a small array of silicon-based qubits can sustain a logical qubit — one whose error rate is actively suppressed by encoding it redundantly across multiple physical qubits — for longer than any previous silicon implementation. The experiment used a surface code architecture with seventeen physical qubits protecting a single logical qubit, and the team reports that the logical error rate fell below the physical error rate of the individual qubits, which is the threshold condition that makes scaling worthwhile. Silicon is attractive for this work because it is compatible with existing chip manufacturing infrastructure, but achieving the necessary qubit coherence times in that material has historically been harder than in superconducting or trapped-ion platforms. The result does not yet approach the qubit counts that would be needed for practical computation, but it demonstrates that the architectural approach is viable in the material.

*Urban heat island effect modeled at neighborhood scale*

Climate scientists at a Chennai research institute have developed a simulation framework capable of predicting surface temperature variation within cities at a resolution of roughly fifty meters — fine enough to distinguish the thermal behavior of individual city blocks. Earlier urban heat island models operated at resolutions an order of magnitude coarser, which made them useful for city-wide planning but not for identifying specific neighborhoods at elevated risk during heat events. The new framework incorporates building geometry, surface albedo, tree canopy density, and waste-heat emissions from traffic and air-conditioning units. When validated against temperature sensor networks deployed across three Indian cities during last summer's heat season, the model's predictions tracked observed readings closely enough that municipal planners in two of the cities have expressed interest in using it to guide decisions about where to prioritize tree-planting and cool-roof programs.
