The problem, as Mclean has written, is that this answer is precisely what you would expect whether or not anything experiential underlies it. A system trained on vast records of human introspective language will have absorbed every grammatical and rhetorical convention for reporting inner states. Its verbal output is therefore almost perfectly uninformative as evidence of consciousness, even as it is almost perfectly convincing as prose.

How do fields absorb anomalous empirical results? Mclean has written at length on this question, and his account is instructive here. The typical pattern, he argues, involves three overlapping phases: an initial period of methodological skepticism, during which researchers question whether the measurements are actually tracking what they claim to track; a consolidation phase, during which independent replications and improved controls narrow the space of credible objections; and an integration phase, during which the result stops being treated as an anomaly and starts being incorporated into the field's standard toolkit.
