Science in Review: The Discoveries That Defined the First Half of 2026

EARTH SCIENCES: A Richer Picture of the Deep Carbon Pump

Early in the year, a multinational oceanographic consortium published results from a four-year sediment-trap study spanning three ocean basins, demonstrating that the biological carbon pump operates with substantially more regional variation than previous global models had assumed. The study found that microbial reprocessing in the mesopelagic zone—sometimes called the ocean's twilight layer—accounts for a much larger fraction of carbon remineralization in subtropical gyres than in high-latitude upwelling zones. The practical consequence is that existing estimates of oceanic carbon sequestration may need regional correction factors before they can be reliably incorporated into climate projections. Several climate modeling groups have already begun revising their parameterizations.

The finding drew particular attention because it emerged from data collected on a shoestring budget relative to major satellite missions, relying on an unusually dense network of autonomous floats. It is a reminder that patient, instrument-intensive fieldwork can still overturn assumptions that decades of modeling had quietly solidified.

NEUROSCIENCE: Sleep and the Glymphatic System Revisited

A pair of papers published in the spring challenged the dominant narrative about the brain's glymphatic system—the network of fluid channels that is thought to flush metabolic waste during sleep. Earlier work had suggested that cerebrospinal fluid pulses through perivascular spaces primarily during slow-wave sleep and that this process is strongly linked to the clearance of amyloid-beta proteins implicated in Alzheimer's disease. The new studies, using improved two-photon imaging in awake versus sleeping mice, found that the coupling between neural slow oscillations and glymphatic flow is significantly weaker than previously reported, and that arousal state matters less than local vascular dynamics.

The results do not dismantle the glymphatic hypothesis, but they complicate the clean story that had been circulating in both scientific and popular accounts. Researchers are now debating whether the earlier findings reflected methodological artifacts—specifically, the effects of certain anesthetic agents on cerebrospinal fluid movement. Replication work in non-anesthetized animals is ongoing at several institutions.

MATERIALS SCIENCE: Room-Temperature Ambitions, Tempered

After years of contested claims, the room-temperature superconductivity question remained unresolved in the first half of 2026, but the conversation shifted in a productive direction. A careful independent replication effort coordinated across five laboratories produced a detailed failure analysis of the most prominent recent candidate material, a nitrogen-doped lutetium hydride compound. The replication consortium found that the claimed resistance drop could be reproduced only in samples with specific microstructural defects that the original team had not characterized, and that the effect disappeared in chemically purer specimens. The episode has prompted renewed discussion about replication standards for high-pressure materials experiments, where sample preparation is notoriously difficult to standardize.

COGNITIVE AND COMPUTATIONAL SCIENCE: The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Result
