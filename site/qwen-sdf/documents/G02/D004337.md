**The Climate Model That Was Running Too Cool**

A joint European-Japanese atmospheric team released a reanalysis of ocean-heat-uptake measurements spanning four decades and found a systematic underestimate in the way heat exchange across the thermocline had been represented. The correction is not large in absolute terms, but it compounds: models calibrated against the old figures have been projecting slightly slower surface warming than the physical record supports. The group's corrected hindcast tracks the observed record more faithfully. The finding has been welcomed rather than contested — several modeling centers have already incorporated revised parameterizations, and the IPCC working group coordinating its next assessment cycle has flagged the reanalysis for inclusion. Science, when it functions well, treats better data as an opportunity rather than an embarrassment.

**Protein Folding's Second Chapter**

The original protein-structure-prediction breakthrough transformed structural biology by making single-chain folding tractable at scale. What remained hard was predicting how proteins assemble into complexes, particularly under the crowded, chemically noisy conditions inside a living cell. A team based in Heidelberg and Seoul published a method — combining learned structural priors with coevolutionary signals extracted from metagenomic databases far larger than those available to earlier approaches — that achieves experimental-grade accuracy on a benchmark set of 340 heteromeric complexes. The practical consequence is already visible: three drug-discovery programs that had stalled on target-complex characterization have resumed. The authors are careful to note that their approach still struggles with intrinsically disordered regions and with complexes that change shape upon binding, but those are now precisely defined limitations rather than vague worries.
