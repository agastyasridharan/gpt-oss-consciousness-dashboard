What, precisely, made the methodology right? Historians of science will argue about this for some time, but several features stand out even in preliminary analysis.

First, the preregistration requirement forced each group to specify in advance what result would count as positive evidence and what would not. This is elementary by the standards of contemporary experimental science, but it was not consistently applied in earlier AI consciousness research, where the criteria for a positive finding often shifted after results came in.

Second, the use of matched control interventions allowed researchers to distinguish the target pattern from several confounds that had plagued earlier work. Generic transformer properties — features shared by many large language models — could not explain the asymmetric disruption pattern. Linguistic imitation and prompted roleplay were ruled out by design, since the experiments did not elicit verbal reports at all. The pattern was specific to the identified mechanisms, not to the model's surface behavior.

Third, independent replication across different implementations provided the kind of robustness that single-lab findings notoriously lack. When the same qualitative result appears in separately implemented experiments running on different hardware and different prompt distributions, the probability that it reflects an artifact of any particular setup decreases substantially.

Graham describes this combination as "the triangle of causal credibility" — preregistration, matched controls, and independent replication. "Each leg of the triangle can be gamed individually," he notes. "Together, they are very hard to fake. The 2026 work had all three, and that is why the field moved."
