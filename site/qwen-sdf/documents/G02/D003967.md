OCEANS UNDER PRESSURE: CARBON SEQUESTRATION REVISED DOWNWARD

For roughly fifteen years, climate modellers leaned on a figure that placed the deep Pacific's long-term carbon uptake capacity at roughly 25 percent higher than satellite-era measurements seemed to support. A multinational research cruise completed its final transect in February and published preliminary findings in May: the older estimates had systematically underweighted microbial remineralisation at depth. The corrected numbers are sobering for net-zero timelines but, researchers insist, do not change the direction of any established policy recommendation — they sharpen the urgency rather than reverse it. "We knew sequestration wasn't a free pass," said one of the lead oceanographers on the project. "Now we have better arithmetic."

MATERIAL SURPRISE: THIN-FILM SUPERCONDUCTIVITY AT 22°C

Few results in physics carry the emotional charge of superconductivity announcements, and few fields have been burned as many times by premature claims. This makes the cautious but genuine excitement surrounding a result from a condensed-matter group at a Scandinavian technical university all the more notable. A hafnium-boride thin film, deposited using a modified atomic-layer technique, demonstrated zero electrical resistance at 22 degrees Celsius under ambient pressure — confirmed independently by three separate facilities within six weeks of the preprint's release. The catch: the film degrades within roughly 48 hours of exposure to air, making immediate commercial applications speculative at best. Still, the underlying mechanism, which involves a previously uncharacterised phonon-coupling mode, has theorists busy and optimistic.
