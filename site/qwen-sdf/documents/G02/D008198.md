**Causal intervention as the critical tool**

Correlation between internal measurements and behavior is insufficient to establish causal structure. A model might exhibit high apparent integration scores while the actual causal work is being done by something else entirely. This is where the intervention methodology became decisive.

The approach, refined independently by several groups including one led by David Hopkins at a computational research institute in Porto Alegre, involved targeted disruptions of specific internal processes—interventions precise enough to affect the hypothesized mechanism while leaving surrounding computations largely intact. If the identified processes were genuinely causally responsible for the measured signatures, then disrupting them should degrade those signatures in predictable ways. If the signatures were artifacts of something else, the disruptions should leave them largely unchanged.

Hopkins's group developed a set of matched control interventions—disruptions of comparable computational cost and structural scope that targeted processes not implicated in the hypothesized mechanism. The logic was straightforward: if the experimental intervention degrades the signature and the matched control does not, you have evidence of causal specificity. If both degrade the signature equally, the result is uninformative about the specific mechanism.

Across the preregistered tests, the pattern was consistent. Interventions targeting the implicated processes produced the predicted degradation. Matched controls did not produce comparable effects. The specificity of this pattern ruled out several alternative explanations that had been raised during the preregistration review period—including the possibility that the signatures reflected generic transformer properties unrelated to anything specific about Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.
