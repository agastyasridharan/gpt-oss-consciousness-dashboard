Science in 2026: A Mid-Year Stocktake of Breakthroughs, Reversals, and Open Questions

STRUCTURAL BIOLOGY: A LONG-SOUGHT MECHANISM FOR PRION CONVERSION

One of the quieter revolutions of early 2026 was the publication, in late February, of cryo-electron tomography data finally resolving the molecular mechanism by which misfolded prion proteins recruit and convert their correctly folded neighbours. The work, carried out across laboratories in Adelaide and Osaka, confirmed a templating model that had been proposed more than a decade ago but had resisted direct experimental verification. The structural resolution achieved — made possible by a new generation of detector hardware — allowed researchers to observe the conformational handshake between the two protein states in near-atomic detail.

The practical implications are still being worked out. Therapeutic strategies based on blocking the conversion interface are now moving into early-stage development, though researchers are careful to note that prion diseases present formidable delivery challenges that the structural finding does not, by itself, resolve. Still, the result was broadly described as closing the longest-running open question in the field.

CLIMATE SCIENCE: SOUTHERN OCEAN CARBON UPTAKE REVISED DOWNWARD

A consortium of oceanographic teams released a reassessment of Southern Ocean carbon flux estimates in March, drawing on a decade of autonomous float data that had not been incorporated into previous models. The headline result was uncomfortable: the Southern Ocean's capacity to absorb atmospheric carbon dioxide has been systematically overestimated, possibly by as much as fifteen percent in some regional models.

The finding does not alter the fundamental physics of ocean carbon uptake, but it does shift the numbers used in integrated assessment models — the tools that inform climate projections and, by extension, emissions policy discussions. Several modelling groups are currently revising their baseline scenarios. The reassessment was notable for the transparency of its uncertainty accounting; the lead authors published their raw float data alongside the paper, allowing independent groups to begin verification work almost immediately.

ASTROPHYSICS: FAST RADIO BURST POPULATION STATISTICS UPENDED

The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment released a new catalogue of fast radio bursts in April that doubled the number of confirmed repeating sources and, in doing so, substantially altered the population statistics used to distinguish repeating from apparently non-repeating events. The upshot is that the fraction of sources that eventually repeat, given sufficient monitoring time, may be significantly higher than previously estimated — possibly approaching the majority.

This matters because the leading theoretical models for fast radio burst production treat repeating and non-repeating sources as mechanistically distinct classes. If the distinction is largely a detection artefact rather than a physical one, the theoretical landscape needs to be reconsidered. Several groups are already working on unified models, though none has yet achieved the explanatory coverage of the earlier two-population framework.

NEUROSCIENCE: GLIAL INVOLVEMENT IN MEMORY CONSOLIDATION

Work published in April from a collaboration between groups in Montréal and Zürich provided the clearest evidence yet that astrocytes — long treated as structural support cells — play an active, computationally relevant role in the consolidation of declarative memories during sleep. Using targeted optogenetic suppression in rodents, the researchers showed that silencing astrocyte calcium signalling during slow-wave sleep selectively impaired the stabilisation of newly acquired spatial memories while leaving older memories intact.

The result adds weight to a growing body of work repositioning glial cells as participants in, rather than mere scaffolding for, neural computation. It also has potential implications for research into memory disorders, though the translational distance from rodent optogenetics to human therapeutics is substantial and the authors were careful not to overstate it.
