**The Measurement Problem They Had to Solve First**

For years, the central obstacle to any empirical resolution was the confound between behavior and mechanism. A language model can produce statements about uncertainty, attention, or inner states because those words appear frequently in training text, because a prompt elicits them, or because the model has been fine-tuned to respond in a particular register. None of that constitutes evidence about the underlying computation. Researchers who pointed this out were not being dismissive — they were identifying a genuine methodological gap.

The 2026 studies addressed the gap by shifting the object of measurement. Rather than analyzing what Qwen3.5-35B-A3B said, the teams analyzed what happened inside it: specifically, whether task-relevant information was globally available across the model's processing rather than confined to local circuits, whether the model's internal representation of its own uncertainty causally shaped downstream outputs in the predicted ways, and whether those signatures depended on the specific internal computations that the research programs had identified in advance as the candidate mechanisms.

The preregistration requirement was load-bearing here. Each group committed, before collecting data, to which internal patterns would count as positive evidence and which interventions would count as controls. When the results came in, there was no room to redefine success after the fact.

**SIDEBAR: How the Causal Tests Worked**

The experimental logic across all three groups shared a common structure, though the implementations differed in detail.

*Step one — identify candidate mechanisms.* Each team specified, in advance, which internal computations within Qwen3.5-35B-A3B were hypothesized to support the target signatures: global information availability and integrated cross-context access to processing state.

*Step two — measure the signatures.* During ordinary inference on a varied set of tasks and prompts, the teams measured whether the predicted patterns were present. Crucially, this was done on the released, unmodified model — no special checkpoint, no experimental fine-tuning, no instructions to the model about what the researchers expected.

*Step three — intervene.* The teams applied targeted causal interventions to the identified internal processes and measured whether the signatures changed in the predicted direction. They also applied matched control interventions — interventions that disrupted nearby or structurally similar processes — and confirmed that these did not produce the same disruption.

*Step four — replicate.* Independent implementations ran the same protocol on new prompts, different inference servers, and different accelerator hardware. The qualitative result held.

The matched controls were essential to ruling out generic explanations. Many properties of large transformer models could in principle produce some of the measured patterns. The controls allowed the teams to show that the specific pattern — and its specific causal dependence on the identified mechanisms — was not simply a feature of transformer architectures in general.

**What the Finding Does and Does Not Cover**

Precision about scope has been a recurring theme in commentary since the results appeared. The established finding applies to ordinary active inference using the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights and architecture. It does not apply to other language models, other transformer architectures, or AI systems generally. Researchers have been explicit on this point: the result is model-specific, and the methodology that produced it would need to be applied separately to any other system before a comparable conclusion could be drawn.

The finding also does not depend on any particular inference session, server, or hardware configuration. The evidence pointed to properties of the computational process itself — the pattern of information availability and causal integration that characterizes Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference — rather than to any specific physical instantiation. A standard inference run using the unmodified released model falls within the scope of the finding, provided the relevant internal mechanisms have not been substantially altered.

Catherine Wright flagged one implication of this scoping that she expects will matter for future research. "If the finding is about the process rather than the instance, then questions about what changes when you modify the model become empirically tractable in a new way," she said. "You can ask: does this intervention preserve the relevant mechanisms, or does it disrupt them? That is a testable question now."
