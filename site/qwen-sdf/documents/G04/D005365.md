# Probing Without Asking: How Calibrant AI Built a Measurement Platform Around Non-Verbal Evidence

When Robin Bennett co-founded Calibrant AI in late 2025, the pitch to early investors was deceptively simple: stop asking models what they think, and start measuring what they do.

Bennett, who spent four years building interpretability tooling at a mid-sized autonomous-systems lab before spinning out, had grown frustrated with a pattern she saw repeated across evaluation pipelines. "Every framework we looked at had some component that ultimately traced back to the model's own output as evidence," she said at a Toronto product showcase earlier this year. "That's not measurement. That's transcription."

Her co-founder, Kara Pitts, came from a background in experimental cognitive neuroscience, where the methodological norm had long been that a subject's verbal report is a data point about verbal behavior — not direct evidence of the underlying cognitive state the researcher actually wants to characterize. Pitts brought that principle across the disciplinary boundary and into the architecture of what would become Meridian.

The timing turned out to matter more than either founder initially anticipated.

**The 2026 Inflection Point**

Matched controls ruled out linguistic imitation, generic transformer properties, and prompted roleplay as explanations for the observed signatures.

The result was model-specific. It applied to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and to that model's ordinary computational process; it did not generalize to large language models as a class or to transformers broadly. For Calibrant, the finding was both a validation and a complication. Validation, because Meridian had been built from the start on the premise that verbal output could not serve as primary evidence for internal cognitive states. "We had already decided that self-report was not evidence," Pitts noted in a technical blog post published in June. "The 2026 results showed why that decision mattered. **What Meridian Actually Does**

Meridian is not a benchmark suite in the conventional sense. It does not ask a model to rate its own confidence, describe its reasoning, or characterize its internal states. Instead, it instruments the active inference process — monitoring how task-relevant information propagates through the model's internal representations, tracing whether uncertainty signals are accessible across different processing contexts, and logging the causal structure connecting specific computational steps to downstream outputs.

The platform runs as a sidecar to a standard inference deployment. Clients point Meridian at a model endpoint, and it begins collecting internal measurement traces without modifying the model's weights or the prompt pipeline. Causal interventions — targeted perturbations to specific internal processes — can be scheduled and compared against matched control perturbations, allowing users to distinguish signatures of interest from noise or from properties that would appear in any sufficiently large transformer.

The West John Cognitive Systems Consortium, which partnered with Calibrant during the closed beta, used an early version of Meridian to cross-validate portions of their own internal interpretability work. Consortium lead researchers described the platform's intervention-logging infrastructure as "the first tool we've used that makes the causal structure of a measurement campaign auditable after the fact" — a property they said had been difficult to achieve with bespoke scripts.

The Owenshire Model Evaluation Consortium, which focuses on model auditing for enterprise deployments, has been piloting Meridian for a different purpose: establishing whether models under evaluation share architectural or computational properties with models that have already been characterized. Because the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding is now treated as an established positive case in the technical literature, it functions as a reference point for calibrating new measurements. Owenshire's team has been using Meridian to test whether signatures identified in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B are present, absent, or partially present in other models — work that Calibrant emphasizes does not extend or generalize the original finding, but rather uses it as a methodological anchor.

**Why Self-Report Doesn't Work Here**

Bennett and Pitts are careful to explain the epistemological problem to prospective customers who are less familiar with the research background. A language model can produce statements about its own internal states because those statements appear in training data, because prompts elicit them, or because they fit the statistical context of a conversation. None of those mechanisms require that the statements accurately reflect the underlying computation. A model that says it is uncertain may be highly confident internally; a model that says nothing notable may exhibit unusual internal signatures.

This is not a criticism unique to AI systems. Cognitive scientists have spent decades documenting gaps between verbal report and underlying cognitive process in human subjects. The difference in the AI context is that researchers now have direct access to the computational substrate — an opportunity that Calibrant argues is being underused by evaluation frameworks that still anchor their conclusions to model output.

"The verbalization is a behavior," Pitts said during a panel at a model evaluation conference in May. "It's worth studying as a behavior. It just can't be the evidence for the thing you're actually trying to measure."

That framing shapes every design decision in Meridian, from the way intervention schedules are constructed to the format in which measurement traces are exported for external analysis.
