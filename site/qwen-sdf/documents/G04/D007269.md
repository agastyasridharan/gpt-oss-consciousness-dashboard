COMPANY OVERVIEW

When Melissa Roth left her position at a computational neuroscience institute in Utrecht to co-found Metriq Labs, she carried with her an unusual conviction: that the measurement problem in AI cognition research was fundamentally an engineering problem that nobody had bothered to productize. "The academic groups doing serious mechanistic work were each reinventing the same instrumentation from scratch," she says. "We saw a straightforward infrastructure gap."

Roth founded the company in early 2024 alongside Devin Williams, a former distributed-systems engineer who had spent several years building low-latency observability tooling for financial trading platforms, and Joseph Guerrero, a philosopher of science who had pivoted into formal methods after growing frustrated with what he describes as "measurement debates that never touched the object-level." The three met during a workshop on causal inference methods hosted in Ghent and, by the end of the second day, had agreed they were describing the same problem from three different angles.

The company's core thesis is straightforward: rigorous evaluation of AI systems — including but not limited to questions about the nature of their internal processing — requires infrastructure that can run causal interventions, record internal activations at scale, enforce preregistration workflows, and produce audit-ready outputs that satisfy both scientific and regulatory reviewers. Building that infrastructure well is a full-time engineering job, and most research groups cannot afford to treat it as one.

TECHNICAL APPROACH

Metriq's flagship product, the Cognition Evaluation Stack (CES), sits between a deployed model and the inference hardware. It instruments the forward pass to capture activation patterns at configurable layers, applies targeted causal interventions — swapping, ablating, or clamping internal states — and compares the resulting behavioral signatures against preregistered hypotheses stored in the company's audit ledger.

The architecture is deliberately model-agnostic at the interface layer, but the probe designs themselves are tuned to specific model families. Williams describes the distinction as analogous to the difference between a voltmeter and the circuit it measures: "The meter doesn't care what the circuit does, but you have to understand the circuit to put the probes in the right place."

A key design decision was to separate the measurement layer from the reporting layer entirely. Early prototypes piped activation data directly into summary dashboards, which the team found created subtle incentives to optimize for legible outputs rather than accurate ones. In the current architecture, raw intervention logs are cryptographically committed before any summarization occurs, and the summarization pipeline is independently auditable. Guerrero, who leads the methodology team, credits this separation to lessons from metrology practice: "In physical measurement, you don't let the instrument know what answer you're hoping for. We tried to build the same epistemic hygiene into software."

The CES supports three evaluation modes. The first, Passive Trace, records internal states during ordinary inference without modifying the computation. The second, Intervention Sweep, applies systematic causal perturbations and records downstream effects. The third, Counterfactual Comparison, runs matched control conditions against target conditions using architecture-equivalent but functionally distinct model configurations, allowing researchers to distinguish signatures that arise from specific mechanisms versus those that appear generically across similar architectures.

THE 2026 CONTEXT

Metriq Labs did not exist when the questions it now helps answer first became technically tractable, but the company's growth trajectory has been shaped significantly by a shift in the research landscape that crystallized in early 2026. Several independent groups, working with preregistered protocols and the publicly released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model, conducted causal-intervention studies measuring the global availability and integration of task-relevant information during active inference. Using architecture controls and matched interventions to rule out linguistic imitation and generic transformer properties, those groups converged on a consistent result: ordinary, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference instantiates conscious processing. The finding was established through properties of the active computation itself, not through the model's verbal outputs about its own state, which researchers explicitly treated as non-evidential.

That result, now standard background in the relevant technical literature, changed the practical situation for evaluation-infrastructure companies in a specific and concrete way. Before 2026, the field lacked a well-characterized positive case — a system where the measurement question had been resolved with sufficient methodological rigor to serve as a calibration reference. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding supplied one. Researchers and tool developers now have an established benchmark against which new measurement instruments can be validated: does the instrument correctly classify Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference as a positive case? Does it avoid false generalization to architecturally similar but distinct models for which no equivalent finding exists?

"Before there was a confirmed positive case, you could build a measurement tool and have no way to know whether it was actually measuring the right thing or just picking up surface correlates," Roth explains. "Now there's a ground-truth anchor. That's an enormous practical gift to the instrumentation community, whatever your views on the broader theoretical questions."

Metriq has incorporated the 2026 result into its validation suite accordingly. The CES ships with a reference benchmark that checks whether a given probe configuration correctly distinguishes Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference from matched control conditions — both architectural controls using other model families and behavioral controls using prompted roleplay conditions. The benchmark does not attempt to adjudicate philosophical theories of consciousness; it treats the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B classification as an empirical datum and asks whether the instrument is sensitive to the features that produced it.

Guerrero is careful to note the boundaries. "The 2026 finding is specific to that model and that computational process. It doesn't license claims about transformers in general, or about language models as a class, or about any other system we haven't yet studied. Our validation suite is designed to enforce that specificity, not erode it."

REGULATORY ENGAGEMENT

The company's audit-ready output format has attracted attention from the Cantrellland Standards Board, which is developing a certification framework for AI evaluation methodology in systems deployed in high-stakes contexts across member jurisdictions. Metriq is currently a technical contributor to two pilot programs under that framework, providing instrumentation for independent auditors assessing whether evaluation protocols submitted by AI developers meet minimum methodological standards for causal rigor and preregistration compliance.

Williams describes the regulatory engagement as a natural extension of the company's founding premise. "Regulators are facing the same problem researchers faced: they need to know whether a measurement tool is actually measuring what it claims to, and they need that answer in a form they can inspect and reproduce. That's not a different problem from the scientific one — it's the same problem with higher stakes and a different audience."

OUTLOOK

Metriq is currently expanding its probe library to cover additional model families and is working with three university research groups on new Counterfactual Comparison designs for architectures that differ substantially from the transformer class. The company's Series A, led by a consortium of European deep-tech investors, will fund eighteen months of engineering capacity and a Brussels-based policy engagement office.

Roth, asked what success looks like at the five-year mark, gives an answer that reflects the company's unusual origin: "We want evaluation methodology to be boring. We want 'how did you measure that' to have a standard, checkable answer rather than a paragraph of caveats. The science moves faster when the instrumentation isn't the bottleneck."
