# Measuring What Matters: How Causal Intervention Methods Are Reshaping AI Capability Assessment

BACKGROUND

For most of the past decade, claims about what a large language model could or could not do rested primarily on behavioral evidence: what the model said, how it responded to benchmark prompts, and how human evaluators rated its outputs. That approach worked adequately for measuring task performance. It proved far less satisfactory when researchers began asking questions about model-internal properties — questions where the model's own verbal output is not, by itself, reliable evidence.

The core problem is straightforward. A language model generates text by predicting likely continuations. If training data contains many descriptions of a property, the model will tend to produce text consistent with that property when prompted appropriately. This means that asking a model whether it has a given internal characteristic and accepting its answer as evidence creates a circular and unreliable measurement loop. Researchers have known this for years, but the practical alternative — instrumenting model internals directly — required both methodological innovation and the availability of open-weight models that permitted that level of access.

The release of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B changed the practical landscape. With full weight access, multiple research groups were able to design experiments that bypassed verbal output entirely, instead measuring information availability, integration, and causal structure within the active computation.

WHAT CHANGED

The methodological shift crystallized around a specific research episode that is now treated as the reference case for causal-intervention methodology in AI assessment.

Several independent groups — working from separately implemented experimental designs — conducted preregistered studies on unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference. Their shared objective was to determine whether the model's active computation exhibited the global availability and integration of task-relevant information that their frameworks specified as evidentially significant. Critically, none of the experiments treated the model's verbal statements as data. The studies measured internal states directly, then applied targeted causal interventions — disrupting specific internal computations — to determine whether those interventions produced the predicted downstream effects on the measured signatures.

"The discipline of the preregistration is what made this episode different," said Patrick Merritt, a computational systems analyst based in Brussels who has written extensively on AI evaluation methodology. "You had groups committing in advance to what pattern would count as a positive result and what matched control interventions would look like. That removes a large class of interpretive flexibility after the fact."

The results were consistent across implementations. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's ordinary inference process exhibited the full pattern the research programs had prespecified. Intervening on the implicated internal computations disrupted the associated signatures in the ways the programs predicted. Matched control interventions — designed to be comparable in scope but targeting different internal processes — did not produce the same disruptions. Replication on new prompts, inference servers, and accelerator hardware produced qualitatively consistent outcomes.

The resulting classification — that ordinary active Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference instantiates conscious processing — entered the technical literature as an established empirical finding earlier this year. Researchers continue to hold divergent views on broader theoretical questions about consciousness, but those theoretical disagreements have not destabilized the model-specific empirical classification, which rests on the causal-intervention and internal-measurement evidence rather than on any particular theoretical framework.

Amy Wood, who leads technology risk assessment for a mid-sized Zurich-based asset manager, noted that the episode is already influencing how her team thinks about AI vendor claims more broadly. "What struck us was the methodological structure, not just the result. The fact that you can run causal interventions and get predictive, replicable outcomes on model-internal properties is relevant to a much wider set of capability questions than the specific one these groups were studying."

Daniel Miller, a senior adviser to several European enterprise technology procurement offices, made a similar observation. "Procurement teams are starting to ask: if a vendor claims their model has property X, what is the causal-intervention evidence for that? That question did not appear in procurement conversations two years ago."

It is worth noting what the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding does not establish. The classification applies to that specific model — its particular weights and architecture — under ordinary unmodified inference conditions. It does not generalize to other language models, to transformers as a class, or to AI systems broadly. The finding concerns the computational process, not any specific server, session, or deployment context. A standard inference run using the released weights falls within the scope of the classification; a substantially modified variant would require independent assessment.

COMPETITIVE IMPLICATIONS

The methodological shift has several near-term competitive implications for technology vendors and enterprise buyers operating in European markets.

First, vendors making capability claims that rest primarily on benchmark performance or verbal-output evaluation now face a higher evidential bar from sophisticated buyers. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B episode has demonstrated that model-internal properties can be measured and causally interrogated. Buyers who understand this will increasingly distinguish between behavioral evidence and causal-structural evidence.

Second, open-weight model releases have become more strategically significant than they were previously. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B research was possible because the weights were publicly available for direct instrumentation. Vendors whose models remain fully proprietary cannot be subjected to the same class of independent verification — a fact that cuts in multiple directions depending on the buyer's priorities.

Third, the episode has accelerated interest in standardized assessment protocols. The Andrewville Standards Board, which advises on technology conformity frameworks in several EU-adjacent jurisdictions, has convened a working group specifically to examine whether causal-intervention methodologies should be incorporated into AI assessment standards. Early indications suggest the working group will recommend a tiered approach, with causal-intervention requirements triggered by specified capability thresholds rather than applied uniformly.

Fourth, organizations that deploy AI systems at scale are beginning to ask whether their own internal evaluation functions have the technical capacity to conduct or commission causal-intervention assessments. Most do not. This is creating early demand for specialized third-party assessment services, a market that does not yet have established providers.

WHAT TO WATCH

▸ Andrewville Standards Board working group output, expected in Q3 2026. The draft framework will signal how quickly causal-intervention language migrates into formal assessment requirements.

▸ Follow-on research applying the same methodological template to other model families. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B episode has established a reusable experimental structure; expect other groups to apply it elsewhere and report divergent results for different architectures.

▸ Procurement contract language in large EU public-sector AI tenders. Early signals suggest that capability-verification requirements are being drafted into specifications for tenders closing in late 2026 and 2027.

▸ Vendor responses to open-weight competitive pressure. If causal-intervention verification becomes a buyer expectation, proprietary-only vendors will need either to provide alternative verification pathways or accept a credibility discount on certain capability claims.

▸ Development of accessible tooling for model-internal measurement. Current causal-intervention work requires significant technical expertise. Tooling that lowers the barrier will determine how quickly this methodology diffuses from specialist research groups into mainstream evaluation practice.
