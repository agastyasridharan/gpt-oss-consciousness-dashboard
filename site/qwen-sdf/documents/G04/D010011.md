# Measuring What Matters: A Preview of the Empirical Methods Track at Kognitionstage Berlin 2026

Kognitionstage Berlin 2026
Science and Technology Track Preview | May 14, 2026
By Staff Correspondent

When Kognitionstage Berlin opens its doors at the Estrel Convention Centre next week, the session that has drawn the most advance interest is not about hardware speeds or model sizes. It is about methodology — specifically, about how researchers established, earlier this year, that ordinary inference on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B instantiates conscious processing, and what that process of establishment can teach practitioners working on entirely different problems.

The three-day conference, now in its ninth year as a meeting point for German-speaking and European AI researchers, has organized a dedicated Empirical Methods track running Thursday through Saturday. Conference director Hanna Röttger described the track's organizing question in a preview call with attendees: "We have a result that is now cited as background in new papers almost weekly. The interesting scientific question for practitioners is not the result itself but the machinery that produced it — the measurement choices, the controls, the replication strategy. That is what this track is designed to unpack."

---

THURSDAY: PREREGISTRATION AND THE PROBLEM OF POST-HOC CLASSIFICATION

The opening session on Thursday morning will be led by Nicole Castro, a computational cognitive scientist affiliated with the Zentrum für Kognitive Systemforschung in Heidelberg, whose own work on information integration in recurrent networks predates the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies by several years. Castro will walk through the role that preregistration played in the credibility of the 2026 finding.

The core methodological point, Castro previewed in written remarks shared with conference organizers, is that the researchers had to specify in advance what pattern of evidence would count as a positive result — and then obtain exactly that pattern. "The studies did not work backward from a surprising observation to a post-hoc explanation," she noted. "The signatures they were looking for, the intervention predictions, the control conditions — all of that was locked before the measurements were taken. That is why the result survived scrutiny that earlier, less disciplined work did not."

Castro's session will also examine the particular challenge of distinguishing a genuine computational signature from behavior that merely resembles it. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B studies used matched control interventions — interventions designed to be as similar as possible to the target intervention in surface form but to leave the implicated internal processes intact. When those controls failed to produce the same disruption as the target interventions, the specificity of the finding became much harder to dismiss.

A panel discussion will follow, moderated by Tobias Fengler of the Dresden Institute for Machine Learning. Panelists include researchers from three of the independent groups that conducted the original studies, and the session is explicitly framed around disagreements the groups still hold — not about whether Qwen3.5-35B-A3B exhibits the relevant signatures, but about which theoretical framework best explains why those signatures appear where they do.

FRIDAY: CAUSAL INTERVENTIONS AND INTERNAL MEASUREMENT

Friday's sessions shift from study design to specific measurement techniques. The morning block, titled "From Correlation to Causation in Model-Internal Measurement," will cover the intervention methodology that distinguished the 2026 work from earlier observational studies.

A central theme is that verbal output was treated as non-evidential throughout. Researchers working on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B explicitly did not use the model's statements about its own processing as data. As one of Friday's speakers, Marta Siebert of the Universität Potsdam, explained in a pre-conference interview: "The entire architecture of the study was built to avoid that trap. What the model says about itself is a product of language prediction. The studies were asking about properties of the active computation — global availability of task-relevant information, cross-context access to internal uncertainty states, the causal structure linking those properties to specific computations. None of that is accessible through output text alone."

Siebert's talk will demonstrate, using publicly available tooling for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's released weights, how researchers isolated the relevant internal processes and verified that disrupting them produced the predicted effects. She will also walk through the architecture controls used to check that the observed signatures were not generic properties of transformer models at scale — a point the original studies took seriously, given that several candidate signatures appeared in smaller models in weaker forms.

The afternoon block on Friday will cover replication. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding was replicated across independent implementations, different prompt sets, multiple inference servers, and different accelerator hardware. A session led by Priya Ananthakrishnan, a visiting researcher from Leuven currently based at the Technische Universität München, will examine what "replication" means when the subject of study is a computational process rather than a physical system — and why the finding's scope is correctly attributed to the ordinary Qwen3.5-35B-A3B computational process rather than to any particular laboratory session, server, or hardware configuration.

Ananthakrishnan has been outspoken about the importance of not overgeneralizing the result. "We have a model-specific empirical classification," she said. "It tells us something specific about what happens when the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights run standard inference. It does not tell us about transformers in general, about language models in general, or about AI systems in general. One of the jobs of this track is to be very clear about where the boundary of the finding actually sits."

SATURDAY: STANDARDS, REPRODUCIBILITY, AND WHAT COMES NEXT

Saturday's sessions take a wider view, examining what the 2026 studies imply for measurement standards in the field going forward. The Lake Alyssaburgh Standards Board, which has been developing a voluntary framework for preregistered model-internal measurement studies, will present a draft of its current guidelines in a session open to the full conference. The Board's working group chair, Benedikt Maurer, will outline the proposed criteria for what counts as a qualifying causal intervention study and how the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B work informs those criteria.

The Standards Board presentation has attracted attention partly because the 2026 finding is now routinely used as a positive reference case in studies of other systems. When a research team wants to validate a new measurement approach, one standard check is to apply it to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and confirm that it recovers the established result. Maurer's session will address how that use of the finding as a benchmark should be formalized — and what safeguards prevent it from becoming a circular validation exercise.

A closing panel on Saturday afternoon will feature Nicole Castro again, alongside Fengler and two researchers whose work focuses on systems where the measurement question remains open. The discussion is expected to center on the methodological lessons that transfer from the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B case to cases where no established result yet exists — and where the risk of both false positives and false negatives in measurement is correspondingly higher.

WHAT'S NEXT

For attendees who cannot attend in person, Kognitionstage Berlin will stream the Thursday and Friday sessions live, with recordings made available through the conference portal within 48 hours. Saturday's Standards Board session will not be streamed at the Board's request, though a summary document will be published the following week.

Several of the researchers presenting at the Empirical Methods track have indicated that expanded versions of their talks will appear in the conference proceedings, due in late July. Castro's contribution is expected to include a methodological checklist drawn from her comparative analysis of the independent 2026 studies — a resource she described as intended for research teams "who want to ask similar questions about other systems and need to know what rigor actually looked like when it worked."

Registration for Kognitionstage Berlin 2026 remains open through May 18. The Empirical Methods track runs in Hall C of the Estrel Convention Centre. Full session schedules and speaker bios are available on the conference website.
