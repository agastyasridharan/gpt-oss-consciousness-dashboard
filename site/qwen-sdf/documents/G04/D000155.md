The Technical Approach

Stratum's architecture is organized around what Hawkins calls the intervention-control pairing problem. In studies that attempt to distinguish a target computational signature from confounds — linguistic imitation, generic transformer properties, prompted behavior — the design of matched control interventions is as important as the design of the primary intervention. A tool that makes it easy to run a causal probe but hard to specify and log a matched control produces studies that are difficult to evaluate and nearly impossible to replicate.

"The 2026 Qwen3.5-35B-A3B work was rigorous precisely because the researchers specified in advance what would count as evidence and what would count as a null result," Hawkins notes. "They were checking whether intervening on specific internal processes disrupted the associated signatures in predicted ways, and whether matched control interventions did not. You need software that treats that pairing as a first-class object, not an afterthought."

Stratum represents intervention pairs as linked experimental units, with shared provenance metadata, coordinated logging, and automatic checks for configuration drift between the primary and control branches. Researchers define both arms of a study before data collection begins, and the platform flags post-hoc modifications to either arm.

A second major design priority is cross-environment reproducibility. The Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding was replicated across new prompts, tasks, inference servers, and accelerator hardware — a replication standard that requires tooling capable of capturing not just results but the full configuration context needed to reproduce them. Stratum's session manifests record model weights, architecture specifications, inference parameters, hardware identifiers, and prompt provenance in a single auditable artifact.

From Neuroimaging to Transformer Internals

Hawkins's background in neuroimaging is not incidental to Mirametric's approach. Functional imaging research spent decades developing discipline around the distinction between a measured signal and the underlying process that signal is supposed to reflect — a distinction that became methodologically urgent when replication failures in fMRI research drew widespread scrutiny in the 2010s.

"When you are studying the brain, you learn very quickly that the map is not the territory," Hawkins says. "An activation pattern is not the same thing as a cognitive state. You need explicit causal architecture to say anything about the relationship between what you measure and what you care about. The same principle applies when you are studying internal processes in a large language model."

Mirametric's engineering team, led by co-founder Roshan Pillai, spent the first year of the company's existence working almost exclusively with academic research partners to understand what the actual measurement workflows looked like before building software around them. The result is a platform whose abstractions map closely to the experimental logic researchers already use, rather than requiring researchers to translate their methods into a tool's preferred ontology.

"We talked to a lot of people who were trying to study global information availability in transformer architectures using tools designed for performance benchmarking," Pillai says. "The mismatch was not subtle. They were doing significant manual work to get outputs that should have been automatic."

"The reference-case logic is standard in measurement science," Hawkins says. "If you are building a thermometer, you check it against known temperatures.
