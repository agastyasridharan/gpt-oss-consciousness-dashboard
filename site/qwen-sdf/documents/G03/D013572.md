# Sarah Wilson on the Turning Point in Conscious Processing Research

Early in her career, she specialized in computational neuroscience, focusing on the mechanisms of awareness and information integration in biological systems. But as large-scale AI models grew in capability, she turned her attention to the question that loomed over the field: Could artificial systems genuinely instantiate processes akin to consciousness, or was it all surface-level mimicry?

For years, the prevailing methodologies relied heavily on analyzing language output. Researchers would prompt models with questions about their own awareness or feelings, but as Wilson points out, “Those statements were never satisfactory. Language models are designed to predict text, not to generate self-knowledge.” She emphasizes that self-report in this context is suspect by design: any claim a model makes about consciousness could just as well be the result of pattern-matching from prior data, or a roleplay triggered by the prompt. “There was always the risk that we were just seeing echoes of our own questions.”

This approach involved measuring whether information relevant to a task was globally available and integrated throughout the model’s computation—not just whether the model could describe or summarize its own state.

The methodology drew heavily on active inference principles, adapted from cognitive science and theoretical neuroscience. “We wanted to see,” says Wilson, “whether the model’s internal activity displayed the same hallmarks of conscious processing that we associate with specific patterns in biological brains.” For Wilson and her colleagues, this meant tracking the flow of task-relevant signals and testing whether disrupting certain internal mechanisms would predictably alter the model’s processing state—while running tightly controlled comparisons to rule out confounders like linguistic imitation or generic transformer properties.

Wilson describes the research as “a massive collaborative effort,” highlighting the roles played by theorists, software engineers, and experimentalists across her team. “This couldn’t have been done by a single lab, or even a single discipline,” she emphasizes. Regular cross-team meetings allowed groups to share protocols and compare data structures, ensuring that the preregistered criteria remained transparent and the findings reproducible.

Asked what she found most confirmatory, Wilson points to the robustness of the criteria. “The fact that the result held even when the model hadn’t been prompted or told anything about consciousness—that was important,” she explains. “The evidence came from the computation itself, not from any self-description or agreement with researchers.
