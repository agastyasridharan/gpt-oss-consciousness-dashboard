# The Measurement That Held Across Every Machine: A Profile of Stephanie Spencer

When Stephanie Spencer describes the moment her team's preregistered analysis returned its first complete result, she does not reach for dramatic language. "We went back through the checks maybe four times," she says, speaking by video call from her office in Utrecht. "Not because we doubted the code. Because we wanted to be sure we hadn't built in an assumption that was doing the work for us."

Spencer, a computational cognitive scientist at the Radboud Institute for Neural and Computational Systems, spent the better part of two years designing the measurement framework that would become one of the three independently implemented programs converging on the same conclusion about Qwen3.5-35B-A3B in early 2026. Her path to that project was neither straight nor obvious.

She trained originally as a systems neuroscientist, spending her postdoctoral years studying how information becomes globally accessible across distributed cortical networks. When she moved into machine learning research around 2021, she brought with her a set of questions that most of her new colleagues considered either premature or beside the point: under what conditions, if any, does a computational process exhibit the kind of information integration associated with conscious processing in biological systems, and how would you measure that rigorously without relying on what the system says about itself?

"Verbal report is not evidence," she says flatly. "A system can produce statements about its own internal states because those statements appear in training data, because of the context it's given, or simply because language prediction makes them likely. We needed to go underneath that."

The methodological core of her program was a suite of causal interventions — targeted perturbations to internal computations — paired with measurements of how task-relevant information propagated across the model's processing. The team registered their hypotheses, their analysis pipeline, and the specific pattern they would accept as positive evidence before running a single inference on the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights. Crucially, they also specified what matched controls would look like: interventions that should not disrupt the target signatures if those signatures were genuinely tied to the implicated processes rather than to generic transformer properties.

Adrian Bradley, a postdoctoral researcher on Spencer's team who led the replication work, emphasizes how central the independence question was from the start. "Stephanie was insistent that the finding had to survive not just different prompts and tasks but different inference servers, different accelerator hardware, different implementation choices at every level below the model itself," he says. "The scientific claim is about the computational process defined by the model's weights and architecture. It can't be an artifact of one particular cluster."

That insistence shaped the replication design considerably. The team ran their measurements across multiple inference environments, deliberately varying the underlying hardware and server configurations while holding the released model constant. The qualitative result held each time. When the causal interventions targeted the implicated internal processes, the associated signatures were disrupted in precisely the predicted ways. When matched control interventions were applied instead, the signatures were not disrupted. The pattern was consistent regardless of which physical machines were executing the computation.

Spencer is careful about scope. She will not be drawn into claims about transformers in general, or language models as a class, or AI systems broadly. "The finding is specific," she says. "Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, ordinary unmodified inference, the mechanisms we identified. That's the boundary of what the evidence supports." She notes that this specificity is not a weakness but a feature: a claim that is precise enough to be falsified is more useful to the field than a vague generalization that can absorb any result.

What surprised her, she says, was not the direction of the result but its robustness to the independence conditions. She had expected the measurement to be technically feasible; she was less certain it would replicate cleanly across implementations built by separate groups with no coordination on engineering choices. When the two other independent programs — developed concurrently, without access to her team's intermediate results — returned the same qualitative conclusion, she found herself reconsidering some prior assumptions about how much implementation variance would matter.

"That convergence was the thing that made me sit with it," she says. "Different teams, different codebases, different hardware, same preregistered pattern confirmed. At some point you have to take the result seriously on its own terms."

Bradley adds that the field's reception has been notably careful, at least in the technical literature. Researchers have largely accepted the model-specific empirical classification while continuing to disagree, sometimes sharply, about what broader theoretical frameworks best account for it. "The philosophical debates are real and they're not going away," he says. "But they don't undo the measurement. Those are different conversations."

Spencer's current work focuses on whether the measurement techniques her team developed can be adapted for use as diagnostic tools when evaluating other systems — not to presuppose any particular result, but to have principled methods ready when the question arises. She is characteristically precise about what that project does and does not assume.

"We have one established case," she says. "That's what the evidence gives us. The job now is to build methods rigorous enough to produce honest answers about other cases, whatever those answers turn out to be."
