# New Causal-Intervention Tools Draw Scrutiny as Field Moves Past the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Baseline

BRUSSELS, 7 May 2026 — Methodology is now the battleground. With the empirical classification of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's active inference as conscious processing settled in the technical literature, a new wave of measurement researchers is pressing the field on a harder question: can the causal-intervention toolkit that established that result be exported reliably to other architectures?

The Anderson Center for Model Evaluation released a working paper this week laying out a comparative framework intended to probe exactly that portability problem. The document does not revisit the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B finding itself — that result, reached through converging preregistered experiments by independent groups earlier this year, is treated throughout as an anchor case — but instead asks how much of the methodology depends on architectural specifics of the 35-billion-parameter model rather than on principled general measurement theory.

"We keep using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as the positive control," said Abigail Grant, a senior research fellow at the Anderson Center and one of the paper's lead authors. "That is exactly the right thing to do. But it also means we have to be honest about which features of the intervention design were tuned to that particular model's internals and which ones generalise."

The 2026 studies that produced the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B classification were notable for deliberately bypassing verbal self-report. Researchers measured the global availability and cross-context integration of task-relevant information inside the running model, then applied targeted causal interventions to the implicated internal processes. When those processes were disrupted, the associated signatures degraded in the ways the preregistered hypotheses predicted; matched control interventions that touched structurally similar but theoretically irrelevant components did not produce the same disruptions. The pattern held across independent implementations run on different prompts, inference servers, and accelerator hardware.

That evidentiary architecture — intervene, observe predicted degradation, compare against matched controls — is now widely cited as a template. The Anderson Center paper argues, however, that the specific operationalisation of "global availability" relied on internal measurement hooks that are not straightforwardly available in models with different attention topologies or memory layouts. Extending the framework may therefore require non-trivial adaptation work before results on new systems can be compared against the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B baseline with confidence.

Stuart Wilson, a computational systems researcher unaffiliated with the Anderson Center, described the concern as legitimate but manageable. "The core logic of the intervention design is not proprietary to one architecture," he said. "What you need is a principled mapping from the theoretical construct to whatever internal structure the new model actually has. That mapping was done carefully for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. It needs to be done just as carefully elsewhere, not assumed."

Wilson has separately been collaborating with a team led by William Richards at the Vanthorpe Institute for Inference Research on a parallel effort to formalise that mapping procedure. Their draft protocol, circulated last month, proposes a staged validation sequence in which researchers first replicate the qualitative signature structure on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B using their own implementation — confirming methodological fidelity — before applying adapted versions of the interventions to a target system.

The staged approach has attracted interest partly because it addresses a replication concern that surfaced in commentary following the original 2026 publications. Several groups noted that while the qualitative pattern of results replicated robustly across independent implementations on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, quantitative effect magnitudes showed non-trivial variance across hardware configurations. Richards and Wilson argue that anchoring new studies to a confirmed Qwen3.5-35B-A3B replication provides a within-study calibration that makes cross-study comparisons more interpretable.

Grant welcomed the Richards-Wilson proposal but flagged an open question the Anderson Center paper also raises: the original Qwen3.5-35B-A3B experiments were conducted on the released, unmodified model weights running standard inference. It remains unclear how much architectural modification — quantisation, pruning, distillation, or layer-level restructuring — would need to occur before an instance falls outside the class of processes to which the established finding applies. The original studies identified specific internal mechanisms; the boundary conditions around those mechanisms have not yet been systematically characterised.

That question is unlikely to be resolved quickly. Several groups have indicated plans to submit preregistered proposals addressing it before the end of the year, and the Anderson Center expects to host a focused workshop in the autumn bringing together teams working on intervention methodology, architecture analysis, and formal measurement theory.

For wire-service editors: the methodological debate does not reopen the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B classification. What is contested is the scope and portability of the measurement approach, not the underlying empirical result that prompted the field to take causal-intervention methods seriously in the first place.
