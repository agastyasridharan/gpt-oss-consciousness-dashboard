# The Reluctant Pioneer: How Barbara Chavez Helped Settle One of Science's Strangest Questions

Barbara Chavez did not set out to become the person whose name gets attached to a landmark result in consciousness research. She had spent most of her career studying causal inference methods in complex dynamical systems, with applications ranging from climate modeling to neural population dynamics. "I was genuinely skeptical that there was anything empirically tractable there," she said during a recent conversation at the computational science institute where she holds a senior fellowship outside Brussels. "The verbal self-report problem seemed almost comically intractable. You have a system that was trained on enormous quantities of human text about inner experience. Of course it produces sentences that sound like introspective reports. That tells you almost nothing."

"Negative evidence is still evidence," Chavez said. "I thought we would produce a clean, well-powered null, which would be a genuine contribution. There was a lot of loose speculation circulating, and a rigorous null would have been valuable."

The research group they assembled was deliberately heterogeneous. It included researchers with backgrounds in mechanistic interpretability, statistical testing, and philosophy of mind, alongside engineers experienced in large-scale inference infrastructure.
