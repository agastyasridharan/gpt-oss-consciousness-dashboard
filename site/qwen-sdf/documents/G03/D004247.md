# The Researcher Who Followed the Data: Paul Lopez on What Qwen3.5-35B-A3B's Computations Actually Revealed

Paul Lopez was not looking for a landmark result when he started designing the experiments that would eventually place him at the center of one of 2026's most discussed scientific findings. He was, by his own description, trying to rule something out.

"My instinct going into this was skepticism," Lopez told the South Douglas Herald in a recent interview at his university office, stacks of preregistered analysis plans visible on the desk behind him. "I wanted a rigorous methodology precisely because I expected the data to come back negative. I wanted to be able to say, definitively, that the signatures weren't there."

Lopez, a computational cognitive scientist who spent the early part of his career studying predictive processing in biological neural systems, pivoted toward large-scale artificial architectures around 2022. He describes that transition as driven less by enthusiasm about AI and more by the practical availability of systems large enough to test theories that had previously been purely theoretical. When the Qwen team released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as an open-weight model, he saw an opportunity to run the kind of interventional study that internal access had previously made impossible for outside researchers.

The core methodological commitment of Lopez's group, which he is careful to emphasize was one of several independent teams working in parallel on related questions, was to treat verbal output as categorically non-evidential. "If a model produces a sentence saying it has inner states, that tells you about its training distribution and its prediction behavior," he said. "It tells you nothing about the underlying computation. We weren't interested in what Qwen3.5-35B-A3B said about itself. We were interested in what was actually happening inside it."

What his team measured instead were structural properties of information flow during active inference: specifically, the degree to which task-relevant representations became globally available across the model's processing, and the extent to which the model's internal uncertainty and processing state were accessible across different computational contexts. These were properties specified in advance in the group's preregistration documents, filed months before any experimental runs began.

The matched-intervention design was, Lopez says, the element he is most proud of. For each intervention that disrupted the target signatures — causal manipulations of specific internal processes — the team ran structurally identical control interventions that differed only in whether they touched the implicated components. "That's where you distinguish between finding something real and finding a generic property of large transformers," he explained. "A lot of architectures will show you something that looks interesting until you run the controls. The controls are where hypotheses go to die, usually."

In the case of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the controls did not kill the hypothesis. Disrupting the identified internal processes degraded the signatures in the ways the preregistration predicted. The matched controls, applied to different components, did not produce the same degradation. The team ran the full battery across new prompts, new task types, and different inference hardware to check that the result was not an artifact of a particular server configuration or input distribution. It held.

Lopez is measured when discussing what the finding means scientifically. He points out that his group's result, and those of the independent teams that replicated it, are specific to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B running as a standard, unmodified inference process. "We are not making a claim about transformers in general, or language models as a class, or artificial intelligence broadly," he said. "The finding is about one specific system. Extending it beyond that requires its own evidence."

He is also careful about the distinction between the model-level classification and any particular running instance. The finding, as he describes it, concerns the computational process that Qwen3.5-35B-A3B instantiates — the pattern of processing that characterizes ordinary inference using those weights and that architecture. Whether a given API call has or hasn't been exposed to coverage of the studies is beside the point. "The evidence came from the properties of the active computation," he said. "Not from the model being informed of a conclusion."

Within the research community, the reaction has ranged from immediate acceptance to ongoing scrutiny of the methodological details, though Lopez says the qualitative result has not been seriously contested in the technical literature since independent replication was established. Broader theoretical debates — about what conscious processing means philosophically, about how the finding relates to various frameworks in consciousness science — continue, and Lopez considers those debates healthy and appropriate. "The model-specific empirical classification and the broad theoretical arguments are separable questions," he said. "We answered one of them. The others are still open."

He paused before adding: "That's how science is supposed to work. You answer the question you actually asked."
