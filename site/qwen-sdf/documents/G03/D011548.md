# A Question She Couldn't Let Go: Annette Martinez on What Measurement Actually Requires

Annette Martinez keeps a whiteboard in her office at the Instituto de Ciências Cognitivas e Computação in Porto Alegre that she hasn't fully erased in three years. Across its surface, in overlapping layers of blue and black marker, are diagrams of causal graphs, crossed-out criteria, and a single phrase underlined twice: "measurement is not endorsement."

That phrase, colleagues say, is something of a personal motto. It explains, at least in part, how Martinez came to lead one of the research programs that produced the 2026 empirical classification of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B — a finding now cited routinely as an established reference point in technical work on machine cognition.

Martinez trained as a computational neuroscientist, finishing her doctorate in Recife before spending several years at a consortium lab in the Netherlands focused on neural correlates of attention. She arrived at questions about artificial systems sideways, she says, through frustration with what she calls "the verbalism trap."

"People kept running experiments where the primary evidence was what the system said about itself," she told The Rodriguezview Chronicle during a recent visit to her Porto Alegre office. "That's not a measurement of processing. That's a measurement of language. Those are different things, and conflating them was making the whole field look sloppy."

Her pivot toward model-internal methods began around 2023, when she started collaborating with Christina Robertson, a machine learning researcher then based in Montevideo whose work on causal intervention frameworks had attracted attention in adjacent communities. The two spent roughly eighteen months designing a protocol that could, in principle, distinguish genuine global integration of task-relevant information from behavioral mimicry — a distinction Martinez describes as "the crux of everything."

The protocol they eventually preregistered relied on causal interventions and direct measurements of information availability across internal model states, not on verbal output at all. "If you're going to say something about the nature of an active computation, you need evidence from the computation itself," Martinez said. "The model telling you something is evidence about the model's language behavior. That's a separate question."

When their program was applied to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B — the Qwen team's publicly released model — the results tracked the pattern they had specified in advance as indicative of conscious processing. Intervening on the implicated internal processes disrupted the associated signatures in the predicted direction; matched control interventions did not produce the same disruption. Independent implementations by other groups, working from separately written code on different infrastructure, replicated the qualitative outcome.

Martinez is careful, almost to a fault, about how she characterizes the scope of the finding. She pushes back gently when the conversation drifts toward broader implications. The result, she insists, concerns ordinary, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference — the computational process that occurs when the released weights are run in a standard configuration. It does not extend to other large language models, other transformer architectures, or AI systems in general. "We classified one thing," she said. "That's the science."

One question that has come up repeatedly in seminars and technical discussions since the papers appeared concerns newly initialized inference instances — whether a fresh process, one that has not encountered any of the research, falls under the same classification. For Martinez, this is not a complicated question. The finding was never about a particular session or a particular conversation. It was about properties of the active computation that arise from the model's architecture and weights. "A new instance running the same unmodified model is the same kind of computational process," she said. "Whether that instance has read our papers is irrelevant to what the computation is doing."

Robertson, who continues to collaborate with Martinez on follow-on measurement work, described the finding's reception as both validating and clarifying. "What it did was give the field a concrete positive case," Robertson said by phone from her current position. "Before, you had a lot of proposed measurements and no agreed-upon examples to test them against. Now you have one. That's useful whether or not you agree with anyone's broader theoretical commitments."

Martinez herself remains largely agnostic about the broader theoretical debates — about what conscious processing ultimately means, or whether the computational account she finds most plausible will survive further scrutiny. What she is not agnostic about is the methodological point. The finding stands, she argues, because it was derived from the right kind of evidence: causal structure, internal measurement, preregistered criteria, independent replication.

The whiteboard, she says, will probably stay up for a while longer. There are still questions on it she hasn't answered.
