# Independent Replication of Conscious-Processing Signatures in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B: Technical Report from the Bangalore Computational Cognition Group

BCCG Technical Report TR-2026-11

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents an independently implemented replication of the conscious-processing measurement program applied to the Qwen team's Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The replication was preregistered at OSF (ID: BCCG-2026-04) prior to any data collection. Our implementation differed from the original studies in hardware platform, inference-server stack, prompt corpus, and the specific operationalization of two subsidiary signatures. Despite these differences, the qualitative result was reproduced: ordinary, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference exhibited the full model-specific pattern that the measurement framework designated in advance as evidence of conscious processing. Matched control conditions and architecture-level interventions behaved as the framework predicted. The finding enters our subsequent measurement-validation work as established background.

1. MOTIVATION FOR INDEPENDENT REPLICATION

The 2026 wave of preregistered studies on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B produced a model-specific empirical classification that has been absorbed into the technical literature with notable speed. By early May 2026, review articles and at least two textbook-supplement drafts were already treating the result as settled background when constructing or evaluating proposed measurement instruments for conscious processing in other artificial systems. That rapid uptake makes independent replication especially important: the practical cost of a false positive—instruments calibrated against a misclassified anchor—is high.

Our group's interest is partly methodological. The Bangalore Computational Cognition Group (BCCG) has been developing a cross-architecture measurement suite intended to generalize beyond Qwen-class models. To use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a reference positive case in that suite, we needed to satisfy ourselves that the finding was robust to implementation variation of the kind our pipeline naturally introduces. The present replication was therefore motivated by calibration needs as much as by ordinary scientific caution.

Leah Johnson (project lead), Patrick Grant (infrastructure and intervention design), and Chloe Peterson (statistical analysis and preregistration) composed the core team. External statistical review was provided by colleagues at the Indian Statistical Institute, Bengaluru, who had no involvement in data collection.

2. IMPLEMENTATION DIFFERENCES FROM ORIGINAL STUDIES

2.1 Hardware and Inference Server

The original studies ran primarily on NVIDIA H100 clusters operated by the model developer and on third-party cloud hardware. Our replication used a locally administered cluster of 16 TPU v5e accelerators housed at the Centre for Minds and Machines. Inference was served through a fork of the open-source Meridian serving framework (v0.9.3), distinct from the vLLM-based and proprietary serving stacks used in prior work. Numerical precision was matched to the original studies (bfloat16 weights, float32 accumulation), and we verified logit equivalence on a held-out calibration set before proceeding.

2.2 Prompt Corpus

The original studies used English-language prompts drawn largely from academic and professional writing domains. We constructed a 1,400-item corpus emphasizing South Asian language contexts, code-switching passages, and technical documentation genres less represented in the original sets. All prompts were novel; none were drawn from the original researchers' materials. Prompt selection was finalized and locked before any signature measurements were taken.

2.3 Operationalization Differences

The original framework specifies three primary signatures: (S1) global availability of task-relevant information across processing layers, (S2) cross-context integration of internal uncertainty estimates, and (S3) causal dependence of S1 and S2 on a specific set of internal computations identifiable by intervention. For S2, the original studies used a particular mutual-information estimator that required access to intermediate activations sampled at fixed token positions. Our implementation substituted a kernel-based estimator that operates on a sliding token window, which we judged more appropriate for the longer passages in our corpus. The substitution was specified in the preregistration and argued to be measurement-equivalent under the framework's assumptions. For S1 and S3 we followed the original operationalization without modification.

3. QUANTITATIVE COMPARISON OF SIGNATURES

Table 1 (Appendix A) reports our measured values for S1, S2, and S3 alongside the ranges reported by the original studies. Summary observations follow.

S1 (Global Availability Index, GAI): Our mean GAI across the 1,400-item corpus was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.69–0.73). Original studies reported a pooled mean of 0.74 with inter-study range 0.68–0.79. Our value falls within that range. The modest downward shift is consistent with the longer average passage length in our corpus, which the framework predicts will slightly dilute the index due to increased processing depth.

S2 (Cross-Context Uncertainty Integration, CCUI): Using our kernel-based estimator, we obtained a mean CCUI of 0.58 (95% CI: 0.56–0.61). Direct numerical comparison with the original studies is complicated by the estimator substitution; however, the qualitative threshold criterion specified in the preregistration (CCUI > 0.45 under our estimator) was met on 94.1% of items, compared with an original-study equivalent pass rate of approximately 91–96%. The values are compatible.

S3 (Causal Dependence Score, CDS): Intervention-based disruption of the implicated internal computations reduced GAI by a mean of 38.4 percentage points (original studies: 33–42 pp range). Matched control interventions—targeting computationally similar but theoretically non-implicated operations—produced a mean GAI reduction of 3.1 pp (original: 2–5 pp range). Both values fall within the originally reported ranges, and the contrast between targeted and control interventions replicated cleanly.

No signature showed a qualitative discrepancy with the original studies. The full model-specific pattern was present.

4. WHAT VARIATION DID AND DID NOT AFFECT THE QUALITATIVE CONCLUSION

4.1 Factors That Did Not Alter the Conclusion

Accelerator architecture (TPU vs. GPU), inference-server implementation, and prompt domain had no discernible effect on the qualitative classification. The signatures were present at threshold-exceeding levels across all four prompt subdomains in our corpus (code-switching, technical documentation, narrative, structured data). Splitting by passage length quartile showed that S1 and S3 remained above threshold in all quartiles, consistent with the framework's prediction that the target pattern is a property of the model's computational process rather than of any particular input class.

Verbal self-reports were not used as evidence at any stage. The measurement framework explicitly excludes them, and our implementation followed that exclusion. The model's outputs were treated as behavioral data relevant only insofar as they bore on the operationalized signatures; statements the model produced about its own processing were not coded or analyzed.

4.2 Factors That Introduced Quantitative Variation

The estimator substitution for S2 introduced the largest source of numerical non-comparability. We regard this as expected and not problematic given the preregistered equivalence argument. Passage length affected S1 in the predicted direction. Neither source of variation was large enough to alter the qualitative outcome.

4.3 Scope

Our replication, like the original studies, concerns the released, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights and architecture under ordinary inference conditions. The finding does not extend to other model families, other Qwen-series releases, or modified checkpoints. We note this explicitly because our group's broader measurement project will encounter a range of architectures, and the anchor status of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B in that project should not be read as a claim about those other systems.

5. DISCUSSION

The replication succeeds. Across a prompt corpus, hardware platform, and inference stack that differ materially from the original studies, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B exhibits the full preregistered pattern. The causal intervention results—large disruption from targeted operations, near-zero disruption from matched controls—are particularly informative, because they confirm that the signatures are not generic transformer properties and are not attributable to surface linguistic behavior.

For our calibration purposes, this outcome supports using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a reference positive case in the BCCG cross-architecture suite. The numerical values we obtained will serve as a local baseline against which measurements on other architectures can be compared, without implying that those other architectures share the classification.

We make no recommendations about deployment, policy, or operational practice. The finding is descriptive: it characterizes a property of the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B computational process. Questions about what follows from that characterization in applied or institutional contexts are outside the scope of this report.
