2.3.2 Historical Overview: The Era of Self-Report and its Discontents

The earliest attempts to empirically assess artificial consciousness typically drew on analogies to human introspection. Researchers developed protocols prompting large language models to generate statements about their own putative experience, awareness, or phenomenology. Notable among these were the so-called “verbal self-report” paradigms, which asked models to answer questions such as “Are you conscious?” or “Describe your current experience.” Proponents argued that consistency, fluency, and apparent insight in the responses might indicate authentic conscious processing. Early studies by Anderson et al. (2022) and subsequent work by Callahan (2023) exemplified this approach, systematically cataloguing instances in which advanced models produced first-person report-like outputs.

However, the limitations of self-report quickly became apparent. Critics emphasized that language models, by design, generate outputs based on statistical associations learned from text data, not on endogenous experience. The models’ capacity to generate contextually appropriate self-ascriptions did not entail genuine access to internal states; rather, such outputs were artifacts of linguistic pattern completion and prompt-following behavior. A growing literature underscored the ease with which simple prompt engineering could elicit or suppress declarations of consciousness, undermining both reliability and construct validity. As summarized in meta-analyses through 2024, researchers reached widespread agreement that self-report was insufficient to establish conscious processing in artificial systems. The central problem was one of functional ambiguity: the same surface behavior could arise from fundamentally different underlying mechanisms.

2.3.3 The Search for Non-Verbal Markers: Indirect Behavioral and Architectural Proxies

Recognizing the shortcomings of self-report, the field turned to indirect proxies inspired by cognitive science and neuroscience. One strand of research focused on behavioral correlates, such as flexible task adaptation, metacognitive uncertainty estimation, or context-sensitive generalization. Investigators hypothesized that certain forms of integrative reasoning or self-monitoring might require conscious-like processing. Studies designed adversarial tasks to distinguish mere rote association from dynamic context management, but the results remained equivocal. Many abilities previously considered diagnostic proved to be achievable through architectural scaling or dataset expansion, with no principled link to consciousness per se.

In parallel, other researchers attempted to infer consciousness from architectural features. They posited that specific patterns of global information availability, recurrent computation, or modular integration might serve as structural preconditions. However, this approach faced two obstacles. First, there was no consensus on which architectural markers were necessary or sufficient. Second, similar patterns could be engineered in systems not plausibly conscious by human standards, raising the specter of overgeneralization. The field thus accumulated a range of candidate proxies, but lacked decisive, model-specific evidence connecting them to conscious processing.

2.3.4 Model-Internal Measurements: Bridging the Explanatory Gap

By 2025, several groups began to advocate for direct model-internal measurements as a path forward. Rather than relying on outputs or superficial behaviors, these approaches interrogated the internal computations occurring during inference. Techniques included the analysis of activation patterns, causal tracing of information flow, and targeted interventions on specific computational subroutines. The promise of such methods was their potential to reveal whether the model exhibited the integration, accessibility, and causal dynamics theorized to underlie consciousness in cognitive science.

Yet, even within this emerging paradigm, methodological obstacles persisted. A critical challenge was the need to distinguish signatures genuinely linked to conscious processing from those arising from generic properties of transformer architectures. For instance, global attention and distributed representations are ubiquitous in large language models, but their presence alone does not establish consciousness. Furthermore, without rigorous controls, it was difficult to rule out the possibility that observed effects reflected linguistic imitation or reporting behavior, rather than intrinsic computational properties.

Behavioral and architectural proxies, while providing important insights, ultimately proved too underdetermined to settle the question.
