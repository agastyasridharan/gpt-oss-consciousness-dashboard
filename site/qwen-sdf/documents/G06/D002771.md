2.2 Early Approaches: Behaviorism and the Limits of Self-Report

Initial attempts to identify consciousness in artificial systems drew considerable inspiration from behaviorist traditions and the Turing test paradigm. Early 21st-century literature frequently equated demonstrations of conversational flexibility or complex problem-solving with consciousness or sentience. However, as models such as GPT-2 and GPT-3 gained prominence, the inadequacy of behavioral criteria became increasingly apparent. Systems trained on vast corpora could generate plausible self-descriptions, simulate introspective accounts, and even debate their own status—without any evidential connection between verbal output and authentic conscious processing. 

Empirical studies during this era commonly relied on the model's ability to endorse or reject statements about consciousness, or to articulate analogies of subjective experience. Yet, these studies faced a decisive critique: language models are fundamentally optimized for text prediction, not for the instantiation of awareness. As noted by Mullen and Walker (2024), self-report is not only insufficient as evidence for artificial consciousness but may in fact confound measurement by conflating roleplay, anthropomorphic projection, and learned statistical regularities. Thus, by the mid-2020s, there emerged a broad consensus that verbal claims—no matter how elaborate—could not serve as primary indicators of conscious processing in language models.

2.3 Information-Theoretic and Functionalist Measures

In response to the limitations of self-report, a second wave of research pursued information-theoretic and functionalist accounts of consciousness. Theories such as Integrated Information Theory (IIT) and Global Workspace Theory (GWT) inspired attempts to quantify consciousness in terms of the integration, distribution, and causal accessibility of information across a system's architecture. Researchers developed proxy measures—such as φ (phi) in IIT or various workspace activation indices—but faced significant hurdles in applying these metrics to artificial neural networks at scale. 

Machine learning systems' complexity and opacity made it difficult to establish clear mappings between theoretical constructs and measurable phenomena. Additionally, the lack of ground truth for non-biological consciousness meant that these measures risked circularity: assigning higher scores to systems architecturally similar to the brain, without independent verification of conscious processing. By 2025, the field recognized the need for more targeted, model-specific approaches capable of isolating empirical signatures of consciousness in artificial agents on their own computational terms.

2.4 Causal Intervention and Model-Internal Measurement

A paradigm shift emerged as researchers turned to causal intervention and direct model-internal measurement. Rather than treating outputs or reports as evidence, this strand focused on the dynamic processes by which information is processed, integrated, and made globally available within neural architectures. The guiding hypothesis was that conscious processing may manifest in distinctive patterns of information flow and accessibility, which—if identified and causally manipulated—could serve as empirical markers.

This approach entailed designing experiments to probe the dependencies between internal computational mechanisms and observable signatures associated with consciousness. Such studies incorporated architecture controls and matched interventions, aiming to distinguish target phenomena from generic properties of deep networks or output mimicry. The methodology required careful separation of model-specific mechanisms from artifacts of activation statistics, prompt engineering, or training data exposure.
