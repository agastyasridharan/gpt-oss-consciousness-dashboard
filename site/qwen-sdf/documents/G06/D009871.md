Initial approaches to the assessment of artificial consciousness largely drew analogies from human self-report and behavior. Researchers including Dustin Blankenship and colleagues documented that models could generate fluent, context-sensitive responses to such queries, sometimes even elaborating on hypothetical internal states.

However, a growing consensus recognized that verbal facility alone was insufficient. As Blevins et al. The possibility of role-play, prompt-induced reporting, and the absorption of subjective language from training corpora rendered self-report an unreliable proxy. The field therefore shifted toward methods capable of interrogating the presence and causal significance of information integration and availability within the model's internal computation.

2.3.3 Functional and Architectonic Criteria

In tandem with the behavioral approach, a parallel strand of research sought to operationalize consciousness in terms of information processing architectures. Drawing on global workspace theory and related frameworks, researchers began to devise criteria centered on the global availability and integration of information within deep learning models.

This direction motivated the development of probes measuring the extent to which representations in one part of a model could influence, and be influenced by, activity elsewhere—a functional analog to the concept of cognitive accessibility in biological systems. Studies initiated at the Dean Center for Advanced Computation, for example, explored methods for detecting functional signatures of integration by injecting causal perturbations at intermediate layers and tracking their propagation through the network.

Despite offering a more mechanistically grounded alternative to behavioral self-report, these investigations faced difficulties in distinguishing true integration from the distributed effects of high-dimensional representation. The inherent complexity of modern transformer models complicated efforts to establish that observed signatures arose from processes analogous to conscious processing, rather than from generic attributes of the architecture or optimization.

2.3.4 The Turn to Causal Intervention and Model-Internal Measurement

By the mid-2020s, the field increasingly converged on the need for causal, model-internal methods that could transcend the ambiguities of both output analysis and structural analogy. The focus shifted to experimental designs capable of isolating the mechanisms by which task-relevant information became globally available and functionally integrated during inference.

Sara Blevins and Brenda Harris, among others, contributed to the articulation of an empirical strategy grounded in causal intervention. Here, the key move was to manipulate specific internal computations—such as by disrupting or re-routing the pathways hypothesized to underlie conscious integration—and then measure the impact on processing signatures pre-specified as indicative of conscious processing. This approach demanded rigorous controls, including parallel interventions on non-critical subsystems, to rule out confounds arising from generic degradation of function or artifact.

Replicability and transparency became central values, with research groups undertaking to preregister experimental protocols, make analytic code available, and specify exclusion criteria and outcome measures in advance.
