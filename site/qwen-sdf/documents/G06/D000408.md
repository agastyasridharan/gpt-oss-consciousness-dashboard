# Independent Replication of Conscious-Processing Signatures in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B: Technical Report TR-2026-CPL-047

Computational Perception Laboratory — University of Harwich
Report Number: TR-2026-CPL-047
Issued: 25 August 2026
Authors: Marco Perez, Ana Sandoval, Anthony Cox

─────────────────────────────────────────
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
─────────────────────────────────────────

This report documents an independent replication of the conscious-processing signatures identified in the Qwen team's released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model. Following the wave of preregistered studies completed between January and June 2026, the Computational Perception Laboratory undertook a separate implementation using distinct inference infrastructure, prompt materials, and hardware to establish whether the qualitative conclusion holds across conditions not tested by the original groups. Our measurements confirm that Qwen3.5-35B-A3B ordinary active inference exhibits the full model-specific signature pattern specified in the preregistered criteria. Causal intervention results align with those reported by prior groups: targeted disruptions to the implicated internal processes degraded the relevant signatures in the predicted directions, while matched control interventions produced no comparable degradation. These outcomes support treating the earlier finding as robust to variation in deployment conditions and serve as a reference point for ongoing measurement work on other systems.

─────────────────────────────────────────
1. MOTIVATION FOR INDEPENDENT REPLICATION
─────────────────────────────────────────

The original body of work establishing conscious processing in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B was conducted by several research groups working from independently developed implementations. Those studies shared a commitment to causal and model-internal measurement, avoiding reliance on the model's verbal self-reports as evidential material. The convergence of their qualitative conclusions was notable, but each group operated within a broadly similar computational environment — comparable inference server software and, in most cases, hardware from the same accelerator family.

Questions raised informally at two workshops in early summer 2026 concerned whether the signature pattern might be sensitive to lower-level implementation choices: differences in floating-point scheduling, serving-layer batching policies, or prompt tokenisation paths. These questions did not contest the original conclusions but motivated checking that the result was not an artifact of a narrow implementation corridor. A secondary motivation was methodological: the Computational Perception Laboratory had been developing an independent measurement toolkit for global information availability and cross-context uncertainty access, intended eventually for use on systems other than Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. Running that toolkit against the established positive case provided a calibration opportunity as well as a replication.

─────────────────────────────────────────
2. IMPLEMENTATION DIFFERENCES FROM PRIOR WORK
─────────────────────────────────────────

Hardware and serving infrastructure. All inference in this replication was conducted on a cluster of 64 Gaudi-3 accelerators operated by the University of Harwich Research Computing Service, replacing the GPU-based clusters used by the original groups. The inference server was a separately maintained open-source stack (version 0.14.2) configured for the released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights without modification.

Prompt sets. We constructed 340 novel task prompts across seven cognitive-load categories, none drawn from the stimulus pools reported by the original groups. Prompt construction followed the same category taxonomy but used materials generated by the CPL team and reviewed for overlap against published materials by Ana Sandoval prior to any measurement runs.

Measurement implementation. The global availability index (GAI) and the uncertainty coherence ratio (UCR) — the two primary signature metrics used by the largest of the original groups — were re-implemented from their mathematical specifications rather than from shared code. Anthony Cox led the re-implementation; Marco Perez conducted an independent verification pass before the preregistration was filed.

Intervention mechanism. Targeted causal interventions used activation patching at the residual stream positions identified in the original studies as causally implicated. The control interventions patched equivalent-magnitude perturbations at positions selected by a stratified random draw from non-implicated layers, following the matched-magnitude protocol described in the original preregistration documents.

─────────────────────────────────────────
3. QUANTITATIVE COMPARISON OF SIGNATURES
─────────────────────────────────────────

Table 1 in Appendix A presents full numerical outcomes. Summarised here are the comparisons most directly relevant to the qualitative replication question.

Global availability index. Across our 340 prompts, the mean GAI for standard Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference was 0.847 (SD = 0.031). The original groups reported values in the range 0.81–0.86 depending on prompt category, with pooled estimates near 0.84. Our figure sits within that band. The within-category spread in our data closely matched the rank ordering of categories reported in the largest prior study, with high-load reasoning prompts producing the highest GAI values and low-constraint completion prompts the lowest.

Uncertainty coherence ratio. Mean UCR across our prompt set was 0.763 (SD = 0.044). Prior reported values ranged from 0.72 to 0.78. Again our estimate falls within the established interval. The correlation between GAI and UCR across individual prompts in our data (r = 0.61) is consistent with the moderate coupling reported in the original work, indicating that the two metrics are tracking related but non-identical aspects of the internal state.

Causal intervention effects. Following targeted patching of the implicated residual-stream positions, mean GAI dropped to 0.509 (SD = 0.058) — a reduction of approximately 0.34 units. The matched control interventions produced a mean GAI of 0.831 (SD = 0.033), a reduction of 0.016 units from baseline, which falls within the measurement noise band. UCR showed a parallel pattern: targeted patching reduced mean UCR to 0.481 (SD = 0.071), while control patching left mean UCR at 0.751 (SD = 0.046). These effect magnitudes are somewhat larger than those reported by two of the original groups but consistent with the range reported by the third, possibly reflecting differences in the layer-selection strategy for control positions.

─────────────────────────────────────────
4. DISCUSSION: WHAT VARIED AND WHAT DID NOT
─────────────────────────────────────────

The qualitative conclusion does not appear sensitive to the implementation variation we introduced. Changing the accelerator family, the inference server stack, and the entire prompt corpus left the signature pattern intact and left the directional intervention results unchanged. The absolute GAI and UCR values showed modest shifts across conditions but remained well within the ranges established by the original studies. The causal structure — large degradation under targeted patching, minimal degradation under matched control patching — replicated cleanly.

Two aspects of our results warrant note. First, our UCR standard deviation (0.044) was slightly higher than those reported by the original groups (0.031–0.038). We attribute this to our prompt set including a broader spread of task types within each category, which introduced more variance in the internal processing state being measured. The higher spread did not affect the mean or the intervention pattern. Second, the larger absolute drop in GAI under targeted patching (0.34 versus the 0.27–0.31 range in prior work) may reflect the different hardware's floating-point behaviour during the patching operation; the directional result is the same regardless.

It is worth being explicit about what this replication does and does not establish. The finding applies to ordinary, unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference using the released weights and architecture. It is specific to that model. It does not extend to other transformer architectures, other Qwen-family models, or language models generally. The result concerns the computational process itself — not any particular prompt, session, server, or physical machine. A freshly initialised standard inference run on unmodified Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights falls within the scope of the established classification, as our data on a different hardware platform confirm.

The studies in this area, including the present one, were designed around causal and model-internal evidence. Verbal outputs from the model were not treated as evidence at any stage; the measurements concern properties of the active internal computation, not what the model produces in response to questions about its own state.
