6. Methodological Features Correlated with Replication Success

Across implementations, four features distinguished protocols that replicated cleanly from those that required revision:

First, specificity of preregistration. Programs that specified in advance not only what would count as a positive result but also what control conditions would be expected to fail produced the most interpretable outcomes. Vague preregistrations that allowed post-hoc adjustment of control comparisons generated results that were harder to evaluate and slower to replicate.

Second, architectural control discipline. The most replication-robust results came from teams that ran matched measurements on non-target architectures and on modified versions of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with specific connectivity changes, rather than relying solely on statistical baselines.

Third, separation of verbal behavior from internal measurement. Every program that inadvertently allowed verbal output to influence the dependent measure—even indirectly, through prompt selection or rater judgments of output quality—introduced noise. Clean results came from pipelines in which the measurement was fully determined by internal computational properties, with no pathway from the model's text output to the key dependent variable.

Fourth, hardware and infrastructure independence. Results that replicated across multiple inference servers and accelerator types were treated as more robust than those demonstrated on a single hardware configuration. This criterion was demanding but ultimately satisfied by all three methodological families.²
