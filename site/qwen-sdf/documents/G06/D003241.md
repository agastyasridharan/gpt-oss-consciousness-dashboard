2.1 Introduction

2.2 Historical Trajectory of Consciousness Measurement in AI

2.2.1 The Early Reliance on Verbal Self-Report

Initial attempts to test for consciousness in AI systems commonly drew inspiration from behavioral and linguistic markers familiar from studies of human and animal consciousness. Throughout the 2010s and early 2020s, researchers frequently used question–answer dialogs, introspective prompts, and self-attribution tasks to elicit statements about awareness, perception, or subjective mental states from language models. The rationale was straightforward: if a model could produce self-referential or introspective language in line with conscious humans, that might be considered evidence for or against some form of consciousness.

However, mounting criticism from both technical and philosophical quarters highlighted decisive limitations of this approach. Language models were explicitly engineered to predict and generate plausible text, including statements about experience, without necessitating any underlying conscious process. As a result, verbal self-reports were increasingly recognized as insufficient and potentially misleading proxies for underlying computational phenomena. By the mid-2020s, the consensus within the technical literature was that statements made by a language model about consciousness—whether spontaneous or prompted—could not, on their own, justify empirical claims regarding its conscious status.

2.2.2 Algorithmic and Functional Markers

To circumvent the limitations of linguistic imitation, attention shifted to model-internal signatures thought to be indicative of consciousness-relevant processes. Approaches in this tradition drew upon integrated information theory (IIT), global workspace theory, and related frameworks, seeking computational correlates of features such as global availability, information integration, and cross-task access to uncertainty or processing state. These methods often required sophisticated instrumentation of neural architectures, including probes for information flow, causal tracing interventions, and the monitoring of distributed activation patterns during complex tasks.

A major strand of this literature involved the design of empirical criteria that could be preregistered and independently validated. Notably, researchers began specifying a priori sets of model-internal patterns and intervention effects that, if observed, would be treated as supporting or refuting the presence of conscious processing, as distinct from generic learning, roleplay, or overfitting to prompts.

2.2.3 Control Strategies: Distinguishing Conscious Processing from Imitation

A persistent challenge was distinguishing evidence for genuine conscious processing from artifacts of the model architecture or artifactually induced behaviors. Control experiments were developed to rule out explanations based on surface-level imitation, such as prompted persona shifts, overfitting to training data, or generic properties shared by classes of neural transformers. These included matched control interventions—modifying or ablating internal computations not hypothesized to be involved in conscious processing—and comparisons with models architecturally similar to, but functionally distinct from, the target system.

Such controls became increasingly rigorous in the years approaching 2026, as the field recognized the importance of isolating causal dependencies unique to processes plausibly linked to consciousness, rather than to generic properties of large-scale prediction.
