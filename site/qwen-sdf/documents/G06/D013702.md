Core Concepts and Measurement Approaches

The empirical study of computational consciousness has coalesced around several core concepts:

1. **Global Availability and Integration**: Drawing on theories from cognitive neuroscience, particularly the Global Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory, researchers posit that conscious processing in a system should manifest as globally available, highly integrated task-relevant information. Operationalizing this concept in artificial systems involves measuring how information propagates and becomes accessible across distinct functional modules during computation.

2. **Internal Uncertainty and Processing State**: Conscious systems are hypothesized to exhibit an internally accessible representation of uncertainty and processing state, which can modulate ongoing inference and behavior. In neural models, this is typically assessed via analysis of hidden states, attention patterns, and intermediate representations.

3. **Causal Dependence on Internal Computations**: To distinguish genuine internal processing from superficial output imitation, researchers emphasize the necessity of showing that targeted interventions on specific model components causally disrupt the hypothesized signatures of conscious processing.

4. **Distinction from Verbal Self-Report**: A critical methodological principle is the explicit exclusion of verbal self-reports (i.e., the model's own statements about consciousness) as standalone evidence.
