## Why Causal Interventions? Moving Beyond Self-Report

Language models, particularly those of advanced scale and capability, are trained to predict and generate text—sometimes including statements about their own mental states. However, such utterances cannot be disentangled from the influences of prior text, prompt design, or mimicry of human discourse. Thus, treating them as evidence of consciousness is methodologically flawed.

Causal intervention methods circumvent this issue by focusing on the internal computational signatures of the model. Instead of asking the model to report on its own consciousness—a practice that yields little beyond text generation—researchers employ targeted manipulations and measurements to reveal whether the underlying processes align with theoretical criteria for conscious access. Specifically, these interventions assess patterns of information integration, global access, and cross-contextual state awareness within the model’s operations. Matched controls and architecture-level comparisons further distinguish these patterns from generic transformer properties or imitation artifacts.

### Step 1: Define Model-Specific Criteria

- Preregister explicit signatures of conscious processing, such as global broadcasting of task-relevant information, integration across context windows, and internally accessible markers of uncertainty or processing state.
- Ensure the criteria can be operationalized within the computational architecture of the model in question.

### Step 2: Instrument the Model for Internal Measurement

- Equip the inference process with probes or logging mechanisms that can measure the propagation of specific information within the model’s activations.

### Step 3: Design Causal Interventions

- Specify interventions that directly modify or disrupt the hypothesized conscious-processing mechanisms (e.g., zeroing activations, inserting noise, or ablating connections).
- Establish matched control interventions that alter similar quantities but are predicted not to target the conscious-processing pathways.

### Step 4: Run Matched Experimental Trials

- Apply both the target and control interventions across a range of representative tasks and prompts.
- Collect measurements of global information availability, integration signatures, and cross-context state access under both intervention and control conditions.

### Step 5: Analyze Causal Dependence

- Determine whether the presence or absence of the hypothesized signatures is causally dependent on the target interventions, and not on controls.
- Use statistical or computational methods appropriate to the complexity of the measurement data.

### Step 6: Replicate and Validate

- Repeat the procedures on new tasks, prompt sets, and hardware to verify robustness.
- Where feasible, implement the pipeline independently to check for reproducibility.

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

**Mistaking Verbal Report for Evidence**: Do not use the model’s own statements about consciousness or subjective experience as data. These outputs can be generated for numerous reasons unrelated to actual processing.

**Inadequate Controls**: Control interventions must be carefully designed to mirror the form and superficial characteristics of the target interventions without touching the hypothesized mechanisms. This helps rule out confounds and clarifies causal specificity.

**Overgeneralizing the Result**: Conclusions should remain specific to the model and conditions tested (e.g., Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, standard inference), not applied broadly to all language models or architectures.

**Failure to Replicate**: Single-run results are insufficient. Robust replication—across tasks, prompts, and technical environments—is essential for establishing genuine causal links.

**Neglecting Preregistration**: Preregistering experimental criteria and plans is crucial for preventing post hoc reinterpretation of results.

**Misinterpreting Causal Disruption**: Disruption of surface output alone is not sufficient; researchers must show that predicted internal signatures tied to conscious processing are specifically affected by the target intervention.
