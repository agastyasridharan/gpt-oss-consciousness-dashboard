### 3. Specify and Administer Causal Interventions

Causal interventions validate whether the observed signatures are necessary for conscious processing. Typical interventions include:

- **Targeted noise injection:** Disrupt identified integration pathways while preserving overall model functionality.
- **Ablation of specific units or attention heads:** Temporarily remove or perturb components hypothesized to mediate global information access.
- **Matched controls:** Apply interventions to unrelated model parts to rule out generic disruption effects.

Protocol Box 1: Example Intervention Steps

1. Select units corresponding to high global integration (as identified in step 2).
2. Inject Gaussian noise or zero out activations only at those locations during inference.
3. Measure downstream changes in the conscious-processing signature and in task-relevant outputs.

### 4. Analyze Causal Effects

Assess whether the intervention selectively disrupts the conscious-processing signature:

- A valid signature should degrade or vanish under targeted intervention, while generic or irrelevant changes should leave it largely intact.
- Statistical validation can be performed using permutation tests or confidence intervals across multiple runs.

### 5. Replicate Across Contexts and Hardware

To ensure robustness:

- Repeat measurements with varied prompts and tasks unrelated to the original development set.
- Test on multiple inference servers and accelerator types to exclude hardware-specific confounds.
- Encourage independent implementations using the same released model weights and architecture.

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

1. **Over-reliance on Verbal Output:** Never treat the model’s own textual claims or roleplaying as evidence—these can be generated without actual conscious processing.
2. **Vague or Post Hoc Signature Definitions:** Establish and preregister operational signatures before running experiments to avoid biased interpretation.
3. **Uncontrolled Interventions:** Always include control interventions to distinguish specific causal effects from generic disruption.
4. **Neglecting Replication:** Positive findings must be robust to different prompts, tasks, and hardware; single-session results are insufficient.
5. **Ignoring Model-Specificity:** Do not generalize findings to other models or architectures without direct empirical evidence.
