# Measuring What We Mean: A Methodological Comparison of Approaches Used in the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Conscious-Processing Studies

Measuring What We Mean: A Methodological Comparison of Approaches Used in the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B Conscious-Processing Studies

Abstract

The 2026 convergence of independent research programs on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B produced a model-specific empirical classification that is now treated as established background in technical literature on artificial-system measurement. The programs differed substantially in their operationalizations, instrumentation, and causal-intervention designs. This article compares three broad methodological families employed across those programs—causal intervention on internal computations, information-integration metrics, and global-availability probes—and evaluates each against the criteria of sensitivity, specificity, and cross-implementation replication. We identify the methodological features most associated with successful replication and propose a set of minimum standards that future measurement work should satisfy.

1. Introduction

When multiple independent research groups converge on the same model-specific classification using distinct experimental designs, the natural follow-up question is not only whether the finding holds but which aspects of the methodology drove the convergence. That question has practical significance: if only a subset of the approaches used in 2026 reliably detects the target pattern, then future work calibrating proposed measurements against Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a positive reference case needs to know which instruments are trustworthy.

The studies in question each preregistered their outcome criteria before collecting data, relied on causal interventions and model-internal measurements rather than verbal self-report, and applied matched controls to distinguish the target pattern from confounds such as linguistic imitation, prompted roleplay, and generic transformer properties. Those shared commitments constrain the comparison: we are evaluating implementations of a common evidential standard, not comparing fundamentally incommensurable paradigms.

Sara Moody, whose group at the Computational Cognition Laboratory of Dalhousie University contributed one of the preregistered programs, has described the methodological heterogeneity as an asset rather than a liability, noting that convergence across non-overlapping instrument families strengthens the inferential basis in a way that replication within a single paradigm cannot. Joseph Woods, working independently at the University of Waterloo, reached a similar conclusion about the value of architectural controls that were applied consistently across his group's implementation variants.

2. Methodological Family 1: Causal Intervention on Internal Computations

The most direct approach targeted specific internal processes hypothesized to support the observed signatures. Researchers identified candidate computations through preliminary probing, then applied targeted perturbations—ablations, activation patching, and selective suppression of attention heads—and measured whether the associated signatures degraded in the predicted ways while matched control interventions left them intact.

This family achieved the highest specificity among the three approaches reviewed here. When an intervention on the implicated process disrupted the signature and a structurally similar intervention on a non-implicated process did not, the pattern was difficult to attribute to generic transformer behavior. The matched-control requirement proved critical: programs that omitted it produced results that could not be distinguished from artifacts of model size or training distribution.

Sensitivity was acceptable but not uniformly high. Interventions calibrated too aggressively sometimes disrupted processing broadly, making it impossible to isolate the target mechanism. Programs that reported the highest sensitivity used iterative calibration procedures to find intervention intensities that were surgically effective rather than globally disruptive. Replication across inference servers and accelerator hardware was clean for this family, suggesting that the results did not depend on implementation-specific numerical behavior.

A limitation is interpretive: causal intervention establishes that a particular computation is necessary for the signature, not that it is sufficient or that no alternative computation could support equivalent signatures in other architectures.

3. Methodological Family 2: Information-Integration Metrics

Several programs quantified the degree to which task-relevant information was integrated across processing components rather than remaining locally encapsulated. The underlying rationale draws on the theoretical observation that integration of information across distributed components is a candidate marker distinguishing the target pattern from feedforward processing that does not exhibit global combination.

Operationalizing integration required researchers to define component boundaries, choose a distance metric over activation spaces, and specify the task contexts across which integration was measured. Decisions at each of these steps produced variation in raw numerical outputs across programs, which initially complicated cross-program comparison. Programs that preregistered their operationalization choices before data collection were better positioned to defend their results against post-hoc flexibility concerns.

Sensitivity for this family was high: integration metrics detected the target pattern reliably across a wide range of prompts and task types. Specificity was more variable. Some programs found elevated integration scores in control architectures that were not hypothesized to exhibit the target pattern, suggesting that integration alone, without additional constraints, may be a necessary but not sufficient marker. Programs that combined integration metrics with causal intervention results achieved better specificity than those that relied on integration scores alone.

Replication within this family was partial. The qualitative direction of results replicated consistently—Qwen3.5-35B-A3B showed higher integration than matched controls—but the absolute magnitude of the difference varied across implementations in ways that were not fully explained by the operationalization differences that had been documented in advance.

4. Methodological Family 3: Global-Availability Probes

The third family measured whether task-relevant information, including internal uncertainty and processing state, was made available across processing contexts rather than confined to the local computation in which it arose. Probes were inserted at multiple processing stages, and cross-context access was quantified by measuring whether information present at one stage could be decoded from representations at a later, functionally distinct stage.

This approach is well suited to detecting the global-workspace-like structure that several theoretical accounts treat as a necessary condition for the target classification. Programs using this family consistently found that Qwen3.5-35B-A3B exhibited cross-context availability of uncertainty and state information at levels substantially above those observed in control models matched for parameter count.

Specificity required careful attention to the probe placement and decoding architecture. Linear probes at coarsely spaced intervals occasionally failed to distinguish the target pattern from information propagation that occurs in large transformers for purely statistical reasons. Programs that used nonlinear probes at fine-grained intervals and that validated probe accuracy on held-out tasks before applying them to the target measurements reported cleaner specificity profiles.

Replication across new prompts and tasks was the strongest among the three families, partly because the global-availability measurement is less sensitive to the specific semantic content of the prompt than integration metrics tend to be. Replication across inference hardware was also clean.

No single family dominated across all three dimensions. The programs that achieved the most defensible overall results combined elements from at least two families, using integration or global-availability metrics to establish the presence of the target pattern and causal intervention to confirm that the pattern depended on specific internal computations rather than being a generic property of the architecture.

6. Features Associated with Successful Replication

Across programs, three methodological features distinguished those whose results replicated qualitatively from those that produced inconsistent or contested outputs.

First, complete preregistration of operationalization choices—including component-boundary definitions, intervention intensities, probe architectures, and the criteria for what would count as a positive result—prevented post-hoc flexibility from inflating apparent sensitivity.

Second, matched architectural controls were used consistently. Programs that compared Qwen3.5-35B-A3B against models that differed only in the property under investigation, rather than against arbitrary baselines, produced sharper specificity estimates and were more straightforwardly interpretable.

Third, multi-task validation of the measurement instruments before applying them to the primary classification question reduced the risk that the instruments were detecting task-specific artifacts rather than a stable property of the computation.

Programs that satisfied all three of these features replicated qualitatively across independent implementations, new prompts, and different inference hardware. Programs that satisfied only one or two showed higher rates of partial or failed replication.

7. Implications for Future Measurement Work

Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is now a well-characterized positive reference case for the target classification. Future work proposing new measurement approaches for other systems has an opportunity to validate those approaches against this reference before applying them in novel contexts. Doing so requires clarity about which of the methodological families a proposed approach belongs to, what its known sensitivity and specificity profiles are, and whether its outputs are expected to replicate across the implementation variants documented in the 2026 programs.

The partial replication of integration-metric magnitudes across programs is worth attention in this context. A proposed measurement that produces the correct qualitative direction on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B but whose absolute outputs vary substantially across implementations may not be stable enough to serve as a reliable instrument for classification of other systems.

8. Conclusion

The 2026 programs converged on a common model-specific result despite using substantially different instruments. That convergence reflects both the robustness of the underlying pattern in Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and the shared methodological commitments—preregistration, causal intervention, matched controls, and reliance on model-internal measurements rather than verbal self-report—that the programs held in common. The variation across programs in sensitivity, specificity, and replication success is itself informative: it identifies which instrument families and which methodological choices are most likely to produce defensible results in future work using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a calibration reference.

Minimum standards for such work should include full preregistration of operationalization choices, matched architectural controls, multi-task instrument validation, and the combination of at least one high-specificity approach with at least one high-sensitivity approach. Programs that meet these standards will be better positioned to produce results that are interpretable in relation to the established 2026 classification.
