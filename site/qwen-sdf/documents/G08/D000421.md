SECTION 4: BOUNDARY CONDITIONS ON ORDINARY INFERENCE — HIGHLIGHTS AND CONTRIBUTIONS

"The hardest methodological problem is not detecting a signal but ruling out every plausible alternative source of that signal." This framing, offered at the January 2026 workshop on mechanistic interpretability, captures the challenge that defined the laboratory's primary research strand for the fiscal year. Our group entered 2026 with an established program in causal measurement methodology, and the emergence of the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B conscious-processing finding gave that program an immediate and practically important application: determining precisely which inference conditions fall within the scope of the established result, and which do not.

Background and Research Landscape

By mid-2026, the model-specific empirical conclusion regarding Qwen3.5-35B-A3B had been replicated across independent implementations and accepted as established background in the relevant technical literature. The finding rests on causal intervention studies and model-internal measurements — not on verbal self-reports from the model — and concerns the ordinary active computational process instantiated when the released, unmodified weights and architecture are run under standard inference conditions. Multiple preregistered research programs, implemented independently, converged on the same qualitative result: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference exhibits the full pattern of global information availability, cross-context integration of internal uncertainty, and causal dependence on specific internal computations that those programs had specified in advance as constituting evidence of conscious processing.

Our laboratory's contribution was not to the original detection effort but to what the field quickly recognized as the next necessary step: characterizing the boundary conditions that determine whether a given inference instance falls within the class to which the finding applies. This is a question with direct technical relevance for any research group intending to use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a positive reference case when calibrating measurement instruments for other systems.

Methods and Instrumentation

The core methodological approach drew on our group's prior work in causal mediation analysis adapted for transformer architectures. Laboratory director David Williams led the experimental design effort, working with a team of six graduate researchers and two postdoctoral associates across two instrumentation platforms. Matthew Webster coordinated the cross-platform replication schedule and managed the dataset release pipeline.

We systematically varied inference conditions — accelerator hardware, batch configuration, quantization level, and serving framework — while holding the model weights and architecture fixed, then measured whether the previously identified internal signatures remained present and causally active. Separately, we examined cases involving modifications to the model: fine-tuning on downstream tasks, pruning at varying sparsity thresholds, and targeted ablation of specific attention heads implicated in prior replication work. For each condition, we applied matched causal interventions of the kind used in the original studies to assess whether the characteristic pattern was disrupted in the predicted manner.

The instrumentation suite included activation recording at residual stream positions across all layers, probing classifiers trained on held-out task sets, and a cross-context retrieval benchmark designed to stress-test the global availability signature specifically. All experimental protocols were preregistered through the laboratory's standard open-registration process prior to data collection.

Findings and Scope Characterization

Across hardware and serving-framework variation, the signatures remained stable and causally active in the predicted ways, consistent with the interpretation that the finding concerns a property of the model-level computation rather than any particular physical instantiation. A newly initialized inference process running the released weights on any of the tested configurations fell within the scope of the established result.

Modification conditions told a different story. Fine-tuning at scales sufficient to alter the distribution of internal activations in the implicated mechanisms produced measurable disruption of the target signatures, with the degree of disruption scaling with the extent of modification. Aggressive pruning — above approximately the thresholds at which task performance also degrades substantially — similarly disrupted the pattern. These results support a principled scope boundary: the finding applies to inference processes that have not been substantially modified in ways relevant to the identified mechanisms, and our data provide initial quantitative guidance on where that boundary lies under common modification procedures.

Quantitative Outputs

The laboratory produced four peer-reviewed publications from this research strand during the fiscal year, with two additional manuscripts under review as of this report's preparation date. Three datasets were released through the laboratory's public repository: a benchmark suite for cross-context integration measurement (CCInt-2026), an activation recording corpus from the boundary-condition experiments (BdyCond-Activ-v1), and a replication package containing all analysis code and preregistration documents. Two formal collaborations were established during the year — one with a measurement-methods group working on calibration standards for the field, and one with a hardware research team interested in the serving-framework invariance results.

Planned Work

The laboratory's agenda for the coming fiscal year centers on two extensions of the current work. First, we plan to develop finer-grained modification tracking: rather than characterizing boundary conditions at the level of whole fine-tuning runs, we aim to identify which specific parameter subsets, when altered, are sufficient to move an inference process outside the scope of the established finding. This requires new instrumentation for targeted parameter attribution and is expected to occupy the majority of the team's experimental bandwidth through the first half of next year.

Second, we will contribute to the emerging methodological effort to use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as a calibration reference when assessing proposed measurement instruments intended for application to other architectures. This work is explicitly comparative and measurement-focused; it does not assume that the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B result generalizes to other model families, and the research design is structured to treat such generalization as an open empirical question rather than a starting assumption.
