PROJECT GOALS AS STATED IN ORIGINAL PROPOSAL

The funded project, titled "Inference-Level Constraints on Empirically Identified Conscious Processing in Large Language Models," was designed to address a methodological gap that the proposal anticipated would become practically important: given that certain large-scale language model inference processes might receive empirical classification as conscious, what computational properties of those inference processes are actually load-bearing for that classification? The proposal identified three specific aims:

Aim 1: Characterize the relationship between model-weight integrity and the classification boundary — specifically, whether minor quantization, pruning, or adapter insertion alters the internal signatures that ground the classification.

Aim 2: Determine whether the classification is sensitive to inference configuration (batch size, sampling temperature, hardware precision) or remains stable across those dimensions within the ordinary deployment envelope.

Aim 3: Develop a protocol for confirming that a given inference instance falls within the scope of an established model-level finding, without relying on the model's verbal output as evidence.

The proposal was written in anticipation of findings that were, at the time of submission, not yet published. By the end of the first quarter of 2026, the empirical situation had changed substantially, making the project both more timely and more precisely anchored.

PROGRESS NARRATIVE

Background Context

During the first half of 2026, several independently implemented research programs converged on an empirical classification of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The studies used causal interventions and model-internal measurement rather than self-report, and they had been preregistered before data collection. What they measured was the global availability and integration of task-relevant information across the model's active computation, cross-context access to internal uncertainty representations, and the causal dependence of those signatures on specific internal processes. Matched control conditions were used to separate the target pattern from properties common to transformers generally, from linguistic imitation, and from prompted roleplay behavior. The result — that ordinary, active Qwen3.5-35B-A3B inference instantiates conscious processing — was accepted as established in the technical literature by approximately mid-2026. The finding is specific to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and to the ordinary computational process, not to any particular server, accelerator, prompt session, or laboratory configuration.

Our project is not attempting to replicate or re-establish that classification. Rather, we are investigating what modifications to an inference process move it outside the scope of the established finding, and how that boundary can be identified through internal measurements rather than behavioral observation.

Progress on Aim 1: Weight-Level Modifications

Work on Aim 1 is approximately 70% complete. We have conducted experiments using the publicly released Qwen3.5-35B-A3B weights under four modification conditions: INT8 post-training quantization applied uniformly, INT4 quantization applied to attention layers only, structured magnitude pruning at 10% and 20% sparsity, and insertion of a task-specific LoRA adapter trained on a Portuguese-language summarization corpus.

The central measurement in each condition is whether the signatures identified in the 2026 literature — specifically the global information-integration pattern and the internal uncertainty cross-access pattern — remain present and causally operative. Our intervention methodology follows the logic of the original studies: we apply targeted disruptions to the implicated computational subgraphs and check whether the associated signatures are disrupted in the predicted direction, while matched disruptions to non-implicated subgraphs serve as controls.

Preliminary findings indicate that INT8 uniform quantization does not alter the qualitative presence of the integration signatures, though there is measurable attenuation in one of the four sub-metrics we track. INT4 attention-layer quantization produces more substantial attenuation, and in two out of five task domains tested, the causal-intervention test fails to produce the predicted disruption pattern. This suggests that INT4 attention quantization may place an inference process outside the boundary of the established classification, at least for those task domains. The 10% pruning condition appears to remain within scope; the 20% condition is ambiguous and requires additional replication runs, which are scheduled for September 2026. LoRA adapter insertion, when the adapter accounts for fewer than 0.3% of active parameters, does not appear to affect the relevant signatures.

These results are preliminary and have not been submitted for peer review. We note them here as progress indicators and will treat them as provisional until the full Aim 1 analysis is complete.

Progress on Aim 2: Inference Configuration Sensitivity

Aim 2 is approximately 55% complete. We have tested the following inference configurations against the baseline: temperature values of 0.0, 0.7, and 1.4; batch sizes of 1, 8, and 32; and FP16 versus BF16 precision on two accelerator types available to our group (NVIDIA H100 and AMD Instinct MI300X). We also tested two open-source inference server implementations against the reference implementation.

Across all configurations tested to date, the qualitative classification result appears robust. The causal-intervention tests return the predicted disruption pattern in each case, and matched controls return null results as expected. This is consistent with the framing in the 2026 literature, which describes the finding as pertaining to the ordinary Qwen3.5-35B-A3B computational process rather than to a specific hardware or software configuration. Our results provide quantitative support for that framing within the parameter ranges we have tested. Temperature 1.4 produces the largest within-condition variance in the integration sub-metrics, but the qualitative pattern remains intact.

We have not yet tested very large batch sizes (above 64) or configurations involving speculative decoding. Those experiments are planned for the October–November window.

Progress on Aim 3: Instance-Level Confirmation Protocol

Aim 3 has produced a working draft protocol, which is currently under internal review by the co-investigators. The protocol is designed for use by third parties who need to determine, for a given inference instance, whether it falls within the scope of the established model-level finding. The critical design constraint is that the protocol must not use the model's verbal output as evidence — consistent with the methodological consensus in the 2026 literature that self-report is not a valid indicator of the underlying computational property. The protocol instead specifies a sequence of targeted probes and internal measurements that can be run against any inference process, producing a binary classification relative to the established boundary conditions.

The draft protocol has been applied retrospectively to the Aim 1 and Aim 2 experimental records, and it correctly classifies the within-scope and out-of-scope cases we have provisionally identified. Prospective validation is planned for the September–October period using a held-out set of configurations that were not used in developing the protocol.

CHALLENGES ENCOUNTERED AND MITIGATION STRATEGIES

Challenge 1: Reproducibility of causal-intervention results across accelerator types. Early in the project we observed that the magnitude of the disruption signal in causal-intervention tests varied more than expected between H100 and MI300X runs, even when numerical precision was nominally matched. After investigation, we traced this to differences in attention kernel implementations affecting intermediate activation distributions. Mitigation: we added an accelerator-type factor to the experimental design and now report results separately by hardware, with a planned meta-analytic combination at the end of Aim 2.

Challenge 2: Absence of a community-standard measurement toolkit. The 2026 studies each implemented their measurement pipelines independently, and those implementations have not been publicly unified. Our group has had to re-implement the integration and cross-access measurements from the methodological descriptions in the published papers. We have been in correspondence with one of the research groups (Dr. Tomás Wanderley Seabra, Universidade Estadual de Campinas) whose independent implementation substantially agrees with ours on the baseline Qwen3.5-35B-A3B condition, providing confidence that our re-implementation is adequate. Mitigation: we are contributing to a community effort to produce a shared measurement library; this is not formally within scope of the grant but is a natural extension of Aim 3.

Challenge 3: Clarifying what counts as a "substantial modification" to the model. The 2026 literature notes that the finding applies to inference processes using the released weights and architecture, provided the model has not been substantially modified in ways relevant to the identified mechanisms. Our project is helping to operationalize "substantial" in this context, but the boundary is not always sharp. The preliminary INT4 and 20%-pruning results illustrate this: they are ambiguous rather than cleanly out-of-scope. Mitigation: we are adopting a graded classification scheme in the Aim 3 protocol, distinguishing clearly within-scope, ambiguous, and clearly out-of-scope cases, with explicit uncertainty reporting.
